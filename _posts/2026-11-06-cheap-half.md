<h2 class="abstract-label">Abstract</h2>
<p>AI drug discovery puts its effort in the wrong place. Almost all of a drug program's money goes to the clinic. Everything before the first human dose, designing the molecule included, is only about nine percent of the cost. Most programs die on the target, not the molecule: the target turns out not to matter in people, and only a trial can show that. So the money flows to whoever pays for the trials. Within a class of drugs, the latecomer often wins. Chinese drugmakers sell their molecules to buyers who can afford Phase 3. And AI drug companies are caught in a bind: without a pipeline they are priced like a tool, and with one they become just another biotech. Two earlier booms, genome sequencing in the 1990s and IBM's Watson in the 2010s, ended the same way. The technology stayed in use, and the companies valued on it were sold or shrank.</p>
<p>The computation works, but on the cheapest stretch of the process. The decisions that cost real money (which target to pursue, how to design the trial, when to cut losses) rest on experience that nobody has written down or ever scored, and drugmakers keep both those decisions and the data behind them under tight control. Training models on papers and knowledge bases does not solve this either. Many published results cannot be reproduced, the literature is full of filler and some outright fraud, and the reliable data lives in the wet lab. The benchmarks that model companies use to show off their scientific ability amount to little more than knowledge quizzes and leaderboard grinding. The companies worth backing, then, are the ones that can produce their own gold-standard data cheaply and, in the end, make their own drugs. And to tell a hero from a gimmick, look at only two things: real substance and real money.</p>
</section>

## The money goes to the clinic, not to discovery

Almost all of a drug program's money is spent before it earns anything, but once the drug is approved it earns for years. A patent lasts twenty years from the filing date, which usually leaves ten to fifteen years of exclusive sales after approval. Each pill costs next to nothing to make, and insurers and patients rarely turn a drug down because of its price. A program that stops short of approval earns nothing. Anyone selling to drugmakers should start by asking which part of a program's costs their product touches. Most of the cost is in the clinic, that is, in human trials. Trials come in three phases: Phase 1 checks safety in a small group of people, Phase 2 checks whether the drug works in a larger group, and Phase 3 is the large trial that supports approval.

{% include posts/cheap-half/fig-1.html %}

For a program that makes it to the first human dose, everything up to that point costs about US$15.6 million in 2026 dollars (all figures here are in 2026 dollars). That includes target work, screening (sifting through large numbers of compounds for the ones that bind the target), lead optimization (reworking a promising molecule until it is fit to test in people) and the animal studies needed to apply for a trial. Phase 3 alone costs US$118 million, more than seven times as much.

Now imagine a perfect discovery engine. It is free and instant, and for any target it returns the best molecule that exists. Even so, in a program of about US$174 million (the phases in the chart added together), it could save at most US$15.6 million. Some programs do die on chemistry, from a breakdown product in the body that nobody saw coming, say, or a heart side effect that nobody checked. But this engine is already assumed to be perfect at chemistry, and it still cannot touch the thing that sinks most programs: the target turns out not to matter in people. At best, the engine saves about nine percent of the cost and leaves the risk almost where it was.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>What the chart leaves out</summary>

1. **Most of the money goes to programs that fail.** [The report behind the chart](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/ce102c41a7626cd885f7c88a9f2c4807/drug-development.pdf) puts the average out-of-pocket cost (the cash actually spent) at about US$229 million per drug in development. Add the programs that failed and the cost of capital over ten years of waiting, and the figure becomes about US$1.16 billion. Most programs fail along the way, and the approved drugs have to earn that money back for them.
2. **Most approved drugs never become blockbusters.** A blockbuster is a drug that sells more than US$1 billion a year, and drugs like Keytruda, which sell more than US$10 billion a year, are rarer still. Of the 487 drugs the FDA approved between 2014 and 2023, [Evaluate forecasts that 193 (about 39%) will top US$1 billion in annual sales at their peak, and only 17 will top US$10 billion](https://content.evaluate.com/story/making-of-a-blockbuster/page/3).
3. **A cheaper front end does help, but only the side that pays for the clinic gets paid.** The front end is the discovery stage before trials. At the same success rate, more candidate molecules mean more approvals, so a cheaper front end is worth something. But failure is concentrated in the clinic. Every extra candidate means another round of clinical spending, and the payoff goes to whoever puts up that money. A discovery engine that doubles the number of candidates earns its maker nothing if nobody pays for the trials.
4. **These absolute figures run low, but the ratio holds.** The figures in this model are lower than the large-pharma numbers usually quoted. [DiMasi and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2016.01.012) estimate Phase 3 out-of-pocket costs at about US$364 million per drug in development in 2026 dollars (US$255 million in the 2013 dollars they used). [Deloitte's latest count](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-shared/docs/industries/life-sciences-health-care/2026/measuring-the-return-from-pharmaceutical-innovation.pdf), on 2025 data, puts the average cost of taking a drug from discovery to launch at the 20 biggest drugmakers at US$2.67 billion, with the cost of failures spread across the winners. The estimates disagree on the levels, but in every one of them Phase 3 is the most expensive phase, and by a wide margin.
5. **A small share of the cost does not mean a small share of the value.** An input that accounts for just nine percent of a program's spending can still capture far more than nine percent of the revenue, as long as it is scarce enough. Software businesses do this every day. Discovery in drug development cannot, and one reason is that there are too few buyers. Two Phase 3 programs cost roughly US$240 million to more than US$700 million in 2026 dollars. Apart from twenty or so big drugmakers, only about a hundred biotechs can pay for two at once on their own: [72 listed biotechs in the US and Europe have more than US$500 million in annual sales](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-gl/campaigns/life-sciences-securing-value-data-driven-platforms/documents/ey-gl-beyond-borders-biotech-report-06-2026.pdf), and a number of clinical-stage companies hold a lot of cash. Meanwhile, [nearly 7,000 companies develop drugs](https://www.bio.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/The%20State%20of%20Emerging%20Biotech%20Companies%20-%20Investment%2C%20Deal%2C%20and%20Pipeline%20Trends.pdf). Every one of these buyers has its own chemistry team, and none of them is short of molecules.

</details>

## The second drug often wins

A patent is a trade: the company gets a monopoly, and the price is making the molecule public. The company then runs its trials, and the trials tell everyone watching whether the target holds up. So within a class of drugs (drugs that act on the same target count as one class), the first company pays for the discovery, carries the target risk and ends up handing the answer to its competitors. The second company can start from three things that already exist: a validated target (trials have shown it really matters), a published molecular structure and a disclosed clinical endpoint (the measure the trial used to judge whether the drug works). From there it builds a better drug.

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-followers.html %}

In every row, the drug that entered the class later either became the bigger product or beat the first drug in a head-to-head trial (a trial comparing the two drugs directly), and each time the first company had paid for the discovery.

Read with the cost figures above, this should worry anyone selling discovery. If the second drug often wins, then finding a new target is just a cost, ideally one paid by someone else's shareholders. Big drugmakers make their money on portfolio decisions (choosing which programs get funded, such as which validated target to chase) and on running trials well, not on discovery.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>Survivorship bias in this table</summary>

1. **The winners in this table are the industry's biggest products.** Atorvastatin was the fifth statin (a class of cholesterol-lowering drugs), and its own company's executives nearly killed it because they thought the market was already taken. Adalimumab was the third drug against TNF (an inflammation signal), and it went on to become the best-selling drug in history. Zanubrutinib was the third BTK inhibitor (a drug that blocks the activity of the BTK enzyme) and [beat the first one in a head-to-head trial](https://www.pubmed.gov/36395435/). Zasocitinib, which comes up again later, was filed for approval four years after the first TYK2 inhibitor was approved, and still beat it head to head.
2. **The molecule decided the winner, but the company that ran the trials got paid.** Within a validated class, the molecule is the main difference, which is why chemistry is not going away. But in every row the winning molecule came from a chemistry team the winner already had, and the profit went to the company that won the trial.
3. **This table only includes classes where a second drug came along.** Most targets never get a second drug: the first one fails, and nobody touches the target again. And nobody makes a separate table of the classes where a fast follower had nothing to follow. The rule is narrower than it looks. Latecomers do better only when the target holds up, and someone else has usually spent ten years and a billion dollars finding that out.
4. **Following is a strategy for big companies.** It only works if you can afford the trials that following requires, and then have the factories and the sales force to win the market. In obesity drugs, semaglutide from Novo Nordisk (NVO) reached the market first, but [it spent more than 1,000 days on the FDA's drug shortage list, which blunted its head start](https://www.biospace.com/business/obesity-leaders-dig-manufacturing-moats-to-defend-injectable-glp-1-empires). Tirzepatide, from Lilly (LLY), spent about 300 fewer days on the list. Since then Lilly has steadily taken share from Novo Nordisk, and supply may be one reason.

## China sells the molecules, but the buyer still controls the path to approval

If a drug is only worth something once it gets through the clinic, then what is scarce is the ability to get a molecule past the FDA: enough cash to fund Phase 3, a track record of working with the regulator, and data the FDA is willing to accept. Companies that have all three keep most of the profit. Companies that have none of them can only be asset suppliers (sellers of drug candidates, which the industry calls assets): they make molecules cheaply and sell them to companies that can carry them to approval.

Chinese drugmakers now do this at scale. In 2025 they signed [157 out-licensing deals (out-licensing means selling the rights to a drug candidate) with a headline value of US$135.7 billion, up from 94 deals worth US$51.9 billion in 2024](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-01-14/chinese-drugmakers-strike-record-136-billion-in-cross-border-licensing-deals-102403540.html). The average cash paid at signing has more than tripled since 2022. Two things have not changed, though. Most of the headline value is milestone payments (money paid only if later trials succeed or sales targets are hit), and most of that money will never be paid. And it is still the buyer who decides whether the molecule ever gets a chance to reach patients.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>Behind the deal numbers</summary>

1. **Prices are up, but the deals are still built the old way.** By 8 October 2026, the year's cumulative headline value of out-licensing deals had [reached US$139.4 billion](https://www.nextmsc.com/news/chinas-growing-role-in-the-biotechnology-market), more than in all of 2025. Upfront payments (the cash paid at signing) came to US$8 billion in the first three quarters, compared with US$7.2 billion for all of 2025. According to Evaluate, [the average upfront payment in licensing deals for Chinese assets rose from US$52 million in 2022 to US$172 million in early 2026](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/analyst-china-no-longer-bargain-basement-biotech-acquisitions). The business works like this: a company sells a preclinical molecule (one not yet tested in people) for fifty million dollars, about the average upfront in 2022, and after that, each time the molecule changes hands, the value it gains goes to the next buyer. On 30 January 2026, AstraZeneca (AZN) [agreed to pay CSPC (1093.HK) US$1.2 billion in upfront cash](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/astrazeneca-returns-chinas-cspc-47b-obesity-deal) for the rights outside China to eight once-monthly injectable obesity programs, none of which had finished Phase 1. Eight molecules at that price would have fetched around four hundred million dollars, a third of what CSPC got. Pfizer (PFE) [agreed to pay US$1.25 billion upfront for a single bispecific antibody (an antibody drug that binds two targets at once) from 3SBio (1530.HK)](https://www.biospace.com/business/pfizers-china-deal-worth-up-to-6b-eclipses-peers-in-a-frenzied-market), which was [already in several clinical trials in China](https://www.pfizer.com/news/press-release/press-release-detail/pfizer-enters-exclusive-licensing-agreement-3sbio).
2. **Analysts disagree on how much of the discount remains.** By the count of Clarivate (CLVT), [Chinese companies accounted for 32% of global out-licensing deals in the first half of 2025, up from 5% in 2020. Yet upfront payments for Chinese assets were still 60 to 70% lower than for comparable Western assets, and total deal values 40 to 50% smaller](https://pharmasource.global/content/chart/china-now-sources-32-of-global-biopharma-out-licensing-deals-up-from-5-in-2020/). In early 2026, though, an Evaluate analyst [said upfronts for Chinese companies were catching up with deals elsewhere](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/analyst-china-no-longer-bargain-basement-biotech-acquisitions). Either way, the seller's money comes mostly at signing, and what the drug earns once it reaches the market goes to the buyer.
3. **Data from a single country is the weak point.** Sintilimab, a PD-1 inhibitor (a cancer immunotherapy) from Innovent (1801.HK), was licensed by Lilly and had been tested only in China. In February 2022, the FDA's Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee [voted 14 to 1](https://cancerletter.com/regulatory-news/20220211_1/) that the FDA should require additional trials showing that the results apply to American patients before making a decision. The committee only advises, and the vote was about whether to require more trials, not whether to approve the drug. But the FDA's own briefing document listed three conditions under which foreign trial data alone can support an approval, and found that the trial did not meet them. The FDA then rejected the application. [Surufatinib was rejected on similar grounds](https://pharmaphorum.com/market-access-2/fdas-treatment-of-china-developed-drugs-spurs-demands/), even though it came with a US bridging study (a small extra trial run to show that results carry over to American patients). In May 2026, the acting head of the FDA office that reviews cell and gene therapies [said that running the first human trials outside the United States might pay off in the short term, but could backfire in the long run](https://friendsofcancerresearch.org/news/pink-sheet-first-in-china-trials-may-be-counterproductive-for-sponsors-us-fda-cber-official-says/).
4. **The rule is not absolute.** Toripalimab was [approved on 27 October 2023](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/resources-information-approved-drugs/fda-approves-toripalimab-tpzi-nasopharyngeal-carcinoma) for nasopharyngeal carcinoma on the strength of a Phase 3 trial in a single region and a Phase 2 trial in a single country, after being turned down earlier over manufacturing problems. But multiregional trials are now the default expectation, and their cost either falls on the seller or shows up as a discount in the price.
5. **The current test is ivonescimab.** It is a bispecific antibody from Akeso (9926.HK) that targets both PD-1 and VEGF (a growth factor that tumors use to grow new blood vessels), and Akeso has licensed the rights for the United States, Europe and several other markets to Summit Therapeutics (SMMT). [Akeso has reported positive results from three Phase 3 lung cancer trials run only in China, and one of them, HARMONi-A, showed a statistically significant survival benefit (one unlikely to be due to chance). But Summit's application to the FDA rests on HARMONi, a Phase 3 trial run across several regions](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1599298/000159929826000006/a2026_prx0129xfdablaacce.htm), and the FDA's decision is due on 14 November 2026. The FDA had [explicitly asked for a statistically significant benefit in overall survival (how long patients live), and HARMONi did not deliver one](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/summit-therapeutics-updates-ivonescimab-survival-data-ahead-fda-decision-date). A hazard ratio compares the risk of death between the two groups of patients: 1.0 means no difference, and the lower the number, the better the new drug. Among HARMONi's 165 patients in North America and Europe, the figure was 0.98 at the main analysis in April 2025, against 0.79 for the trial as a whole. These patients enrolled more slowly than expected, so at that point their median follow-up was only 9.2 months. In an update released in July 2026, their hazard ratio was 0.76, close to the 0.78 and 0.79 reported for the whole trial in earlier analyses. The FDA now has to decide whether a survival result that falls short of statistical significance is good enough, and whether the results apply to American patients.

</details>

## The pipeline question has no good answer

Sooner or later, every AI drug discovery company, and more and more AI-for-science companies, has to answer one question: should it build its own pipeline (a set of drug candidates the company takes into trials itself)? Both obvious answers are bad. Answer no, and the company is a software business selling a model, which the industry calls a platform. It can publish results on benchmarks (standard tests for comparing models) and even top them, and that is enough to carry it to around the second funding round. Then investors ask where the revenue will come from, and the answer is a handful of research contracts. They had valued it as a software business.

Answer yes, and the company becomes a biotech. A biotech is valued on its pipeline, a pipeline needs clinical money, and so each extra dollar spent on the platform is a dollar taken from the drug candidates that support the valuation. The asset wins this argument every time, because a drug candidate has a clear date (the day the trial reads out) and a platform does not. There is a third answer: sell molecules early to fund the platform. That keeps the company alive, but only as an asset supplier. Schrödinger (SDGR), Recursion (RXRX) and Isomorphic Labs have all answered this question in public.

At the merger the two companies promised about ten clinical readouts (the moment a trial's results come out). Within ten months, Recursion had stopped or paused three mid-stage programs and laid off a fifth of its staff.

{% include posts/cheap-half/fig-2.html %}

Buyers paid only US$82.5 million in cash to partner with the drug discovery company owned by Alphabet (GOOGL), but US$4 billion in cash for a single molecule that had finished Phase 2b (the larger, later stage of Phase 2).

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>The evidence, company by company</summary>

1. **Schrödinger had every advantage and still ended up building a pipeline.** Founded in 1990, it is the best-known computational chemistry software company, and its software was used to design zasocitinib, discussed later. For decades its answer was no. Then it switched to yes and took three wholly owned cancer programs into the clinic, one of which it [dropped in August 2025 after two patient deaths](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/schrodinger-abandons-cdc7-inhibitor-after-therapy-linked-2-patient-deaths). By mid-2025 it was [exploring strategic options for the lead program](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/schrodinger-reports-second-quarter-2025-financial-results), which in practice means looking for someone else to pay for the trials. For 2025 it [reported revenue of US$256 million and an operating loss of US$167 million](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490978/000149097826000010/sdgr-20251231.htm). Its net loss narrowed to US$103 million only because of US$65 million of other income, about three-quarters of it a paper gain on equity investments.
2. **Without a pipeline, an AI company is a high-tech CRO.** A CRO is a contract research organization, the industry's outsourcing firm. Many AI companies do much the same work: they use algorithms to sift through a large number of options for a drugmaker and tell it which few look most promising, which saves the client time. The business is paid by the project, one job at a time, and cannot be built once and sold again and again like software. By the time a drug reaches the clinic, the factory and the market, the buyer holds all the cards. It has the money, the plants and the sales channels, and it can pay once to buy the patent outright or take a license, after which how much the drug earns has nothing to do with the seller. A seller that wants royalties or milestone payments needs a reason for the buyer to share, and if the buyer can build its own AI team or switch suppliers at any time, there is none.
3. **Within a few budget cycles, the pipeline eats the platform's entire budget.** A single small production run for one program costs around forty million renminbi. It is like a chip tape-out (sending a finished chip design to the factory for a trial run): you commit the money, you wait, and only at the end do you learn the result. A company cannot pay for that and a research platform out of the same budget. Two or three budget cycles later, the platform has become an in-house service group, and the company has become a mid-sized biotech with six programs and a few months of cash. Plenty of AI drug companies look like this today, and a better model would not have changed that.
4. **The third answer turns the AI company into an asset supplier, like the Chinese drugmakers.** It still makes molecules, but sells them early and uses the proceeds to fund the platform. Insilico (3696.HK) discovered the cancer molecule ISM3091 with its own generative AI platform and [licensed it to Exelixis (EXEL) in 2023 for US$80 million upfront](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/artificial-intelligence/stockwatch-insilico-ceo-breaks-down-exelixis-deal/), a month after the molecule entered Phase 1. Insilico's chief executive said one reason was an overcrowded pipeline: 31 programs aimed at 29 targets. Chinese drugmakers use a variant of this called a NewCo, in which [investors set up a new company outside China that takes the rights to the asset and pays for the trials](https://www.pharmaceutical-technology.com/features/newco-licensing-models-are-tipping-deals-in-favour-of-chinas-biotechs/). The originating company and the investors share ownership, and the originating company still collects an upfront payment and royalties (a percentage of future sales). Hengrui (600276.SH, 1276.HK) used this route to license its obesity drug HRS9531 (in the same class as semaglutide and tirzepatide) to a NewCo called Kailera Therapeutics. Selling early keeps the platform going. The catch is that the AI company sells at early-stage prices and gives up whatever the drug earns later.
5. **Recursion and Exscientia merged, then cut programs and staff.** They were the two most prominent platform companies in the West, and when they [merged in 2024](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/after-tough-year-exscientia-folds-recursion-create-ai-super-power) they promised about ten clinical readouts within eighteen months. Layoffs began three months later. By the ninth month, [two mid-stage programs had been stopped and a third paused](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/recursion-pipeline-cuts-first-quarter-earnings/747119/). By the tenth, a fifth of the staff had left and research had shrunk to cancer and rare diseases.
6. **Two platform deals together brought in one-fiftieth of what one molecule did.** Isomorphic Labs is Alphabet's drug discovery company. It is built on AlphaFold (a model that predicts protein structures) and led by a Nobel laureate. In January 2024 it signed [its first two partnerships with drugmakers](https://pharmaphorum.com/news/isomorphic-signs-lilly-novartis-3bn-ai-drug-hunt), with a headline value of about US$3 billion, of which US$82.5 million was cash. In December 2022, Takeda (TAK) [bought Nimbus's TYK2 subsidiary](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/nimbus-used-ai-help-develop-125737581.html) for US$4 billion in guaranteed cash plus up to US$2 billion in sales milestones. What it bought was a single molecule, which had [already completed Phase 2b in psoriasis](https://www.schrodinger.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/23_530_TYK2-Case-Study_Mkt_R7-4_Digital.pdf). Nimbus, too, is a computational drug design company. The cash is about fifty times apart, and the plainest difference is that Nimbus had a molecule to sell. Clinical data probably does not explain most of the gap. In the AstraZeneca and CSPC bar, CSPC's lead program had not been tested in people either, yet it still brought in US$1.2 billion in cash, because eight specific molecules came with it.
7. **Isomorphic has not escaped the pipeline dilemma either.** A platform lives on investors' money, because cash from buyers cannot carry it. In May 2026, Isomorphic [raised US$2.1 billion in a round led by Thrive Capital](https://www.biospace.com/business/ai-fueled-isomorphic-bags-2-1b-the-second-largest-biotech-round-ever), bringing its total to about US$2.7 billion, without having disclosed a single pipeline molecule or put anything into the clinic. The company says it will spend the money developing the platform and using it to fill the pipeline and move programs into the clinic faster. The best-funded discovery platform raised US$2.1 billion to turn itself into a biotech.
8. **Most startups cannot name their molecule, and the listed ones are traditional drug companies underneath.** Ask an AI drug discovery startup to name one molecule that its own platform produced and that a competent human team could not have. Most cannot. The business plan has a pipeline slide, and underneath the pipeline is conventional chemistry. The listed ones run as two outfits under one name: an AI front that raises the money, and a traditional drug R&D group in the back that does the work. When money gets tight, the AI group tends to go first, though the Recursion table above cannot show which group was cut first. It only shows that under pressure, the merged company cut programs and a fifth of its people, with layoffs on both sides of the merger.
9. **Two well-known AI drug companies listed in Hong Kong gave opposite answers to the pipeline question.** Call them Company A and Company B. Company A essentially said no. It sells drug discovery services, lab robots and what it calls AI-for-science solutions, to customers in new materials and consumer health as well as pharma. It [raised more than US$750 million in 2025 by placing new shares](https://conventuslaw.com/?p=129124), and that year it [turned its first full-year profit, RMB 134.6 million on revenue of RMB 802.6 million](https://xtalpi-ir-umb.azurewebsites.net/media/tsnngqsl/2026032501035.pdf). But by [its own announcement](https://www.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2026/0303/2026030301645.pdf), its two core businesses, robotics and drug discovery, were still losing money, just less than the year before. The profit came mainly from two places. First, financial assets it holds rose in value by at least RMB 500 million. Second, in 2024 it had booked a large paper loss on preferred shares issued before its listing. Those shares converted to ordinary shares at the listing, so the loss did not recur. Set those two aside and Company A is a services company with a large investment portfolio, earning its revenue from clients. Company B said yes. It [listed at the end of 2025, in what it called Hong Kong's largest biotech fundraising of the year](https://insilico.com/news/ohz9ozx0t1-insilico-medicine-announces-2025-annual). In 2025 it [had revenue of US$56.2 million, an adjusted loss of US$43.8 million, and R&D spending of about 145% of revenue](https://www.chinadailyhk.com/upload/main/pdf/2026/04/17/6a6f6d898d1ebab8985ef1fc3898c6d3.pdf). By its own account, its newest batch of eight heart and metabolic disease molecules [goes after established, high-confidence targets such as GLP-1R (the target of semaglutide) and Lp(a) (a blood particle linked to heart disease), plus a few newer ones, and aims to stand apart on chemistry](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1105110). That is the follower strategy, and a sensible one. Its lead program is the exception: the target is new, and its own platform found it. It expected to [turn a profit in the first half of 2026, mainly from out-licensing and collaboration income](https://www1.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2026/0709/2026070900039.pdf), which is the third answer described above.
10. **Why the AI label helps, and why so few companies will pay for the research.** The AI label helps because it lowers the cost of raising money: once one drug succeeds, investors treat it as evidence that the platform behind it can produce more. Few will pay for the research itself, because anyone can free-ride. Whatever the first company learns about making the models work, everyone else can learn for free just by looking at its results, so the rational move is to sit on the cash and let someone else go first, just as with a second drug.

</details>

## This is the third time around

Twice before, someone has sold this industry a technology that made the front end of discovery much cheaper, with the pitch that drug discovery was now solved: genome sequencing in the 1990s, and Watson from IBM (IBM) in the 2010s. Each time, a company's valuation rested on the idea that the front end was the whole business, and to hold up that valuation, the company had to produce a drug. Human Genome Sciences became a biotech to get one and ran straight into the cost of trials and the risk of the target. IBM's health unit was eventually sold for far less than had been put into it. Both times the technology stayed on as a tool, bought at tool prices by companies already in the industry, and the valuation was gone. BenevolentAI, the best-known name of the current wave, has already been through it. There is more money this time, but the pitch and the business are the same.

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-waves.html %}

In every row, the technology is still in use, while the company valued on it was sold, shrank or was delisted.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>What each wave left behind</summary>

1. **Human Genome Sciences was not hype, and the science held up.** The genome, the patents and the drug were all real: belimumab was the first new lupus drug in fifty years. It just took twenty years to produce a single drug, because in 1990s drug discovery, sequencing was the cheap half, as molecule design is now. The company that patented genes was eventually bought by a company that could run trials, and its management was gone within fifteen days.
2. **BenevolentAI went down the same road, only faster.** It called itself a machine brain for bioscience, raised money at a valuation of two billion dollars and went public through Europe's largest SPAC deal (a SPAC is a listed shell company that merges with a private one to take it public). When investors asked where the drugs were, it built a pipeline. When the pipeline burned through the cash, it cut the pipeline. It laid off staff round after round, announced it would go back to selling the platform, and finally delisted. The whole cycle took twelve years.
3. **The technologies all survived.** Genomics is now the basis for target validation (confirming that a target really is linked to the disease in people) across the industry, and nobody would start a program without it. Natural-language systems that read the literature are in use at every major drugmaker. The molecule in the next section was designed with free-energy calculations.
4. **They misjudged who gets paid for a tool.** The toolmaker does not own what the tool finds: Illumina (ILMN) sells sequencers, but it does not own the drugs discovered with them. If buyers can build the tool themselves, as with chemistry software, the toolmaker gets even less, because a tool cannot sell for more than it would cost the buyer to make one. Schrödinger sells FEP software (free-energy perturbation, the free-energy calculation mentioned in the previous item), yet the money it made from zasocitinib ([a US$111 million distribution](https://ir.schrodinger.com/press-releases/news-details/2023/Schrdinger-Receives-111.3-Million-Distribution-from-Sale-of-Nimbuss-TYK2-Inhibitor-to-Takeda-02-14-2023/default.aspx)) came from its stake in Nimbus, not from the software. Until its first clinical readout, a discovery platform is just a tool valued as if it were a drug company.
5. **So far, the only way out anyone has found is to sell the asset.** Human Genome Sciences did it by getting acquired, and Nimbus by selling a subsidiary. BenevolentAI did not manage it, because by the time it wanted to sell, it had nothing anyone wanted to buy.
6. **Biotech stocks have badly lagged tech.** Using adjusted monthly closes, from the end of 2011 to the end of October 2026, XBI, an index fund of small and mid-sized biotech stocks, rose about 632%, while QQQ, which tracks the Nasdaq-100 and its large tech companies, rose about 1,304%, a gap of nearly 700 percentage points. Before the pandemic the two ran close: from the end of 2011 to the end of 2019, XBI rose about 343%, slightly ahead of QQQ's 315%. Almost all of the gap opened after 2020, the years when AI advanced fastest and AI drug discovery raised the most money. Index returns reflect many things, including interest rates, acquisitions and drug-pricing policy, so not all of this can be pinned on AI, but there is no sign that AI has lifted biotech as a whole.

</details>

## What computation has actually achieved

There are now two kinds of evidence. One is the aggregate: a 2024 count covering every molecule that came from an AI-native biotech and completed a clinical phase. The other is the strongest single molecule that computational design has produced. In aggregate, AI-designed molecules pass Phase 1 far more often than the historical rate, and pass Phase 2 at about the historical rate. Phase 1 asks an engineering question: is the molecule safe at the target dose, and does it behave in the body the way the chemistry predicted? Phase 2 asks a scientific question: does this target actually matter in this disease? The strongest single molecule, zasocitinib, used computation to solve a hard chemistry problem, but against a target someone else had already validated. So far, the computation has helped at the stage where programs rarely die.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>The numbers behind the evidence</summary>

1. **The aggregate.** In 2024, a team at the consultancy BCG [went through every molecule from an AI-native biotech that had completed a clinical phase](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drudis.2024.104009). Of the 24 that finished Phase 1, 21 passed, about 88% (the report gives 80 to 90%), against a historical 40 to 65%. Of the 10 that finished Phase 2, 4 passed, about 40%, against a historical 30 to 40%. The authors' own reading is that AI is good at producing molecules with drug-like properties (absorption, metabolism, safety and the other properties a drug needs). Comparing the midpoints of the ranges, AI-designed molecules beat the historical pass rate on the engineering question by roughly twenty to forty percentage points. On the scientific question, ten molecules are far too few to prove anything, and their pass rate looks like the historical one. The authors also list well-validated targets as one possible reason for the high Phase 1 pass rate, though they argue it is not the whole explanation.
2. **What a doubled hit rate is worth to a buyer.** The arithmetic is simple. If the buyer's own screen succeeds ten percent of the time and a vendor's succeeds twenty percent of the time, the buyer can screen twice. It only needs one hit (one compound that binds the target), and it can afford to screen, so doubling the success rate only halves its work. It is like a coupon from a food-delivery app: people on a tight budget use it, and people with money do not even look at it. That value is real, just far smaller than the business plan says. There are two exceptions. One is time: if a patent filing is waiting on the screening results, speed is worth money. The other is a target that cannot be screened, and only there does a model open up something new. A model earns its keep by getting a hit where there was none, not by raising ten percent to twenty, and nobody has shown that yet.
3. **The best case: zasocitinib.** In 2016, a joint team from Nimbus and Schrödinger set out to make a selective TYK2 inhibitor. TYK2 belongs to the JAK family of enzymes, and the binding sites of TYK2, JAK1, JAK2 and JAK3 are almost identical, so selectivity (hitting TYK2 without touching the others) was the whole challenge. They started with JH1, the catalytic domain (the part of the enzyme that does the actual work), using free-energy calculations to [score about four thousand designs, pick 46 to synthesize, and find nine that met the project's criteria](https://www.drugpatentwatch.com/blog/artificial-intelligence-in-drug-discovery-what-is-realistic-what-are-illusions-part-1-ways-to-make-an-impact-and-why-we-are-not-there-yet/). They then turned to JH2, a regulatory domain away from the catalytic one, and [screened 2.4 million commercially available compounds computationally against a set of protein structure models](https://www.schrodinger.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/23_530_TYK2-Case-Study_Mkt_R7-4_Digital.pdf). That turned up a new kind of starting molecule, which they optimized into zasocitinib. Binding the regulatory domain instead of the catalytic one is what made selectivity possible: [according to Takeda](https://www.takeda.com/newsroom/newsreleases/2026/fda-priority-review-zasocitinib-psoriasis/), the molecule's selectivity over the other JAK enzymes is more than a million-fold. It was a hard, expensive chemistry problem, and the computation solved it.
4. **Up close, the story is less impressive.** It was Bristol Myers Squibb (BMY) that validated the target and found the allosteric pocket (a binding site away from the enzyme's active site). Nimbus came later, working on a target whose risk someone else had already removed, and making a second drug. Its method was physics-based computation, the FEP described earlier, not a generative model trained on chemistry text. Nimbus's own president of R&D has [said plainly](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/nimbus-used-ai-help-develop-125737581.html) that he would not call a compound identified back in 2020 the first AI-approved drug. And the company that supplied the software was Schrödinger, which now runs its own pipeline alongside its software business. What works here needs three things: a target someone else has already validated, a question narrow enough to be solved as a physics problem, and a wet lab (a lab that really synthesizes and tests compounds) that can afford to make and test dozens of molecules in every round.
5. **What FEP calculates, and what it does not.** FEP estimates the difference in binding free energy between two similar molecules binding to the same protein structure. It simulates one molecule gradually turning into the other, accounting along the way for the energy of the water, the protein and the ligand (the candidate molecule). Done well (with enough sampling and a reliable structure), it is accurate to about one kilocalorie per mole, which corresponds to roughly a fivefold difference in affinity (how tightly a molecule binds). That is enough to rank similar molecules and decide what to make next. It does not tell you whether you picked the right target. Nor does it predict whether the compound will be metabolized within an hour, block the hERG channel (a heart ion channel whose blockade carries a risk of arrhythmia), build up in the liver, or work in mice. It assumes you already know how the molecule sits in the pocket, and it quickly becomes unreliable once the chemical change is large, the protein moves, or the structure itself is wrong. It answers one narrow question well. Every other question in the cycle of designing, making and testing compounds still needs an experiment.

</details>

## The knowledge that matters has never been written down

Ask a veteran medicinal chemist, an old hand, what he does in a round of lead optimization, and the answer sounds routine: look at the activity data (test results showing how strongly the molecules act on the target), pick the next batch of analogs (molecules with small changes to their structure), send them off to be made, wait three or four weeks, and look again. But ask what separates an old hand from a novice, and the answer is no longer routine.

The difference lies in a few judgments: what in the data is signal and what is noise, which paths were tried and abandoned before, which few molecules to make next, and when to stop. None of this is in the literature, because the literature records what worked. And the published record is not reliable enough to serve as training data anyway. When Amgen (AMGN) and Bayer (BAYN.DE) tried to reproduce published results before building programs on them, most of those results did not fully hold up. The science benchmarks reported in model launches do not measure these judgments either. They test knowledge recall and machine-learning engineering (MLE), as the items below explain.

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-benchmarks.html %}

The first three rows are what model launches report as scientific ability, and every one of them is graded against an answer that existed in advance. The last row has no answer until a clinical trial reads out.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>What the record leaves out</summary>

1. **What an old hand actually decides.** He has to judge which differences in a structure–activity table (a table of how changes to a molecule's structure changed its activity) are signal and which are assay noise. Test the same compound twice and the results can differ twofold, so a twofold improvement proves nothing. Knowing when a change is real is a knack nobody has written down. He remembers that a scaffold (the core structure of a molecule) was dropped at his previous company because of a toxicity that was never published. Working within a synthesis budget, he picks thirty analogs out of a thousand plausible ones, knowing he will see results only once a month. And he has to decide when a series (a family of molecules built on the same scaffold) is dead. Of everything he does, this decision costs the most and rests on the least evidence.
2. **Failures are not published, and the published record is unreliable.** Failures stay in internal company databases, the most tightly guarded assets in the industry, so a model trained on the public record learns mostly from attempts that worked. Amgen's hematology and oncology team spent years [trying to reproduce 53 landmark cancer papers](https://doi.org/10.1038/483531a) before betting programs on them, and confirmed six. [A Bayer team reviewed 67 of its own in-house projects](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd3439-c1) and found that the published data fully matched its own results in only about 20 to 25% of them. A third replication effort, the [Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology, repeated 50 experiments from 23 high-impact papers](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.71601). For results that were positive in the original papers, the replicated effect sizes (how big a change the experiment measured) were 85% smaller at the median. Of the effects it could evaluate, fewer than half (46%) met most of its criteria for success. Part of the reason is the incentive: every paper needs a gene nobody has claimed yet, so search almost any gene together with the word tumor and you will find a paper linking the two. So a large share of a big drugmaker's internal research is filtering: before a program is bet on, its scientists repeat the published findings and throw out the ones that do not hold.
3. **Who sets the test.** Drug discovery cannot pick its own judge. First the human body judges, then the FDA. Nobody can define a benchmark, win on it and declare the problem solved, the way computer vision and language modeling do every day. Outside grading is slow and costly, and it also makes it hard for the field to fool itself. To train the kind of judgment described above, you would need boring, specific records from inside real programs: the situation at the time, the decision made and what happened next. For each series, such a record would show what was known, which thirty compounds were picked, what data came back and where the series died. It would include the failures (which are most of it) and carry timestamps, so that a model is scored only on what could have been known at the time. Data like this exists only in fragments inside a few dozen companies, and it will not be pooled, because the record of failures is itself a competitive asset. This is an institutional problem, and more computing power does nothing about it.
4. **The science benchmarks in model launches are Kaggle competitions.** A Kaggle competition gives everyone the same fixed dataset and a question, and the organizer holds the answer key. You submit, you get a score right away, and you can submit again. The science benchmarks reported in model launches come in roughly three kinds (see the table above), and each has the same parts: a fixed dataset, a question, and an answer written in advance. [One widely used agent benchmark consists of 75 Kaggle competitions, scored against Kaggle's own leaderboards](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/7e3767db483c942b883eb4f8cfb74e31-Abstract-Conference.html). Some newer tests use real data and real research workflows, which is progress, but every question still comes from a result someone already has. A high score shows a model can find answers that already exist, and a drug program pays only for the ones that do not.
5. **Critics inside the field agree.** The leaderboards most used within drug discovery are built the same way. A researcher who has spent many years in industry cheminformatics (the use of computers to work with chemical data) argues that [the most widely used leaderboards are too flawed to draw conclusions from](http://practicalcheminformatics.blogspot.com/2023/08/we-need-better-benchmarks-for-machine.html). In December 2025, a paper with 56 authors [argued that mainstream science benchmarks test knowledge out of context and overlook what drives discovery: step-by-step reasoning, proposing hypotheses and interpreting observations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15567).
6. **What a real AI-for-science result looks like.** A real result is a prediction about something nobody knows yet, checked afterwards by someone else. In drug discovery, that means predicting that a molecule will bind before it has been made, or predicting that a target matters before the clinical trial, and then letting a lab or a trial grade the prediction. Mathematics already has results like this: [a model raised the known lower bound on the kissing number in 11 dimensions (the largest number of equal-sized spheres that can touch a central sphere of the same size at once) from 592 to 593](https://spectrum.ieee.org/deepmind-alphaevolve). Nobody had found that arrangement before, and anyone can check it. A result in mathematics can be checked by other mathematicians. A result in drug discovery can only be checked in a lab or in patients, which takes months or even years and real money. So model companies report tests that can be scored the same day.

</details>

## You are automating the cheap half

In practice, AI drug discovery now means a model that proposes molecules. That work falls within the nine percent of the cost that comes before the first human dose, and programs usually do not die there. The best existing version of it needed a target someone else had already validated, and the best-funded platform, Isomorphic, got just US$82.5 million in cash from its first two partnerships, while a single molecule that had been tested in people sold for US$4 billion.

By the industry's own economics, the decisions that cost the most money are these: which validated target to follow, whether to buy an outside drug candidate and how much to pay at signing, how to design the trial, and above all when to stop, whether that means stopping a series or a whole program. Each of these decisions is made by a few senior people at a weekly meeting, yet nobody writes down what they knew and what they expected to happen before the answer comes in, so the call cannot be checked against the outcome later. These decisions are rare and expensive, and nobody checks them afterwards.

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-scoreboard.html %}

Neither drug people point to will settle the question soon. The company that designed zasocitinib will not call it the first AI-approved drug. And rentosertib from Insilico Medicine (a lung-fibrosis drug, and the molecule most often described as AI-discovered) has a Phase 3 trial run entirely in China, with results not due until December 2029.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>The missing record, and three tests</summary>

1. **Parts of these decisions have already been modeled and audited.** The dose is set by exposure–response analysis (how the drug's effect changes with the dose and its level in the blood). AstraZeneca has published [a scorecard comparing its results before and after it overhauled how it decides which programs to advance and which to stop](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd.2017.244): the share of candidates that went from selection to the end of Phase 3 rose from 4% to 19%. But the reasoning behind individual decisions is not recorded anywhere. That is no knock on the people making them, whose experience is real and usually beats the alternatives.
2. **Why the record does not exist.** Hospital medicine explains this best, and portfolio meetings (the meetings where a company decides which programs to fund) work the same way. In the clinic, the real basis of a decision is liability: whoever pays if something happens to the patient gets to decide. And the decision rests on a judgment nobody has quantified, such as how bad this kidney is, how high this blood pressure is, and how much this patient's age should adjust the formula. So the decision comes out as "the other center doses at 200, we dose at 150", and the reasoning is never written down. This is street smart, not book smart, and it works. Portfolio meetings run on the same logic: whoever takes the blame if a program fails makes the call, and a written prediction could later be held against whoever wrote it, so it never gets written. Yet the moment someone writes a prediction down before the outcome, this kind of knowledge becomes testable.
3. **These decisions suit a model well.** They are rare and expensive, so being a little less wrong each time is worth a lot of money, and nobody needs an answer in milliseconds. The decisions themselves are already recorded in the minutes, because they are made at a meeting with a fixed agenda. What is not written down is what was known when each decision was made, and recording that only takes a change to the format of the minutes.
4. **The hard part is scoring a decision to stop.** You only ever see the outcome of the decision you actually made, and a series you stopped will never produce data. So a look back after the fact cannot score a decision to stop. Two things can be scored. One is predictions on the series that were continued, registered before the results came in. The other is the judgment of whichever side was followed when the model and the humans disagreed. Settling it takes forecasts written down and dated in advance, not a database.
5. **Nobody has published this test.** Some companies sell pieces of it: trial-outcome prediction, digital control arms (using historical data in place of real control patients), and portfolio analytics. But none has published a time-stamped record that puts the model's calls next to the humans' calls on the same set of decisions, registered before the data read out. Meanwhile, the money and the talent have poured into molecule generators.
6. **The two test cases will not settle it.** Zasocitinib's regulatory outcome arrives in the first quarter of 2027, but the company that designed it does not call it an AI drug. Rentosertib's trial runs at 47 centers in China, and to win US approval it first has to get past the single-country data problem described earlier. That leaves rentosertib as the one real test of AI molecule design, and the result will not arrive until 2029. Even if it succeeds, it answers a question about the nine percent of the budget.
7. **A 2026 Perspective reached the same conclusion.** [A Perspective published in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery in 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41573-026-01496-2), whose authors include the researcher who coined Eroom's law (the observation that the cost of each new drug has kept rising for decades instead of falling), calls AI's clinical impact "disappointingly limited". AI-designed drugs still hit the same Phase 2 wall as everything else. An improvement of a given size is worth most when it raises the clinical success rate, above all in Phase 2. On this view, a model is worth roughly how much it raises the clinical success rate, multiplied by how many programs it can be used on. A molecule can bind its receptor tightly, kill cells in a dish and shrink tumors in mice, and still do nothing in people, or help only a small fraction of them. Only a molecule that shows a statistically significant effect in a Phase 2 trial has earned the right to be called a drug. But academia chases impact factors and grants, so important negative results rarely get published, and companies built around AI prefer a big story. The statistician John Tukey put it this way: an approximate answer to the right question is worth far more than an exact answer to the wrong one.
8. **What would overturn this conclusion.** First, a platform company reaches a Phase 3 readout on an asset it still owns, without turning itself into a conventional biotech along the way to raise the money. That would show the pipeline dilemma can be avoided and the structure described here is wrong. Second, a discovery platform with no clinical assets gets an upfront payment of US$500 million or more. An equity round does not count. It would show that buyers have started to price the method itself, and the fifty-to-one ratio above would be out of date. Third, someone publishes a time-stamped scorecard from inside real programs showing that, on the same budget and with failures counted, a model beats experienced people at the decisions listed above. It is the cheapest of the three: a company that already holds these meetings could register every prediction before the results come back and publish the scorecard when the program ends, at no extra experimental cost. Still, nobody has published one.

</details>

## Papers are unreliable, and reliable data lives in the wet lab

Much of AI for science today means having a model read papers, patents and knowledge bases, then distill patterns and propose hypotheses from that text. The trouble is that the text itself is riddled with holes. In the life sciences, a large share of published results cannot be reproduced, a good deal is padding, and some is outright fraud. Most knowledge bases are in turn extracted from those same papers. However much a model reads and however well it summarizes, what it learns is that someone wrote this down, not that it is true. Reliable data lives in the wet lab: measurements made with one protocol and one batch of reagents, with the negative results kept and every result time-stamped. Data like that sits mostly with drugmakers and the few companies that run their own experiments, and that second group is what the next section says is worth backing.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>How unreliable the literature is</summary>

1. **Fraud has become an industry.** [More than 10,000 research papers were retracted in 2023, a record, and more than 8,000 of them came from journals of a single publisher, Hindawi](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-03974-8). The retracted Hindawi papers had already been cited more than 35,000 times. A [2025 study in PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2420092122) found that the output of suspected paper mills (operations that mass-produce fake papers and sell authorship) is doubling every year and a half, while the scientific literature as a whole doubles only every fifteen years.
2. **Retractions catch only a small part of the problem.** [A screen of 20,621 biomedical papers found problematic duplicated images in 3.8% of them, at least half suggesting deliberate manipulation](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/mbio.00809-16), and that study checked only one kind of error. [An economic analysis estimated that more than half of preclinical research is irreproducible, costing about US$28 billion a year in the United States alone](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.1002165). The Amgen and Bayer replication efforts described earlier point the same way: most published findings do not fully hold up when someone tries to build a program on them.
3. **One paper can lead a field astray for nearly two decades.** [A 2006 Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04533) reported that a specific amyloid assembly, Aβ*56, impairs memory, and it became one of the most cited papers in Alzheimer's research, steering a great deal of later work and funding. In June 2024 it was retracted after investigators found manipulated images, making it the most cited paper ever retracted. Any model that learned the field from its literature learned that result as fact.
4. **A model that reads the literature learns its errors too.** Text cannot tell a model which results were fabricated, which were never replicated, and which experiments failed and were never written up. A knowledge graph built from papers turns a claim into an edge, and a claim cited a hundred times looks a hundred times more certain, even when every citation traces back to the same flawed experiment. More reading only adds more copies of the mistake.
5. **Why wet-lab data can be trusted.** Wet-lab data is not free of noise either: as noted earlier, the same compound tested twice can differ twofold. The difference is that the noise can be measured. The protocol is fixed, the controls run alongside, the failures are recorded, and any result can be run again. That is also why Lilly's TuneLab and J&J's antibody design challenge, both covered in the next section, insist on data measured in their own labs.

</details>

## What is worth investing in

Drugmakers are reluctant to hand over much of their internal data. They would rather partner with others and build their own models on their own data. Since outsiders cannot get drugmakers' data, the companies worth backing are those that can generate their own gold-standard data (the most trusted experimental data, the kind used as a benchmark for everything else) quickly and cheaply, in a field where drugs can actually be made.

That rules out a large part of the field. Companies selling AI as a software service, or building general-purpose biology models, will have a hard time, because frontier model companies such as Anthropic already work with big drugmakers, and drugmakers can get those services straight from them. Proprietary data counts for more than a proprietary model, because a company can iterate on its data again and again and eventually turn it into drugs of its own. Put another way, the company worth backing is one graded by its own wet lab and, in the end, by its own clinical trials. Lilly's TuneLab, discussed below, is a drugmaker placing this bet itself.

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>What practice shows</summary>

1. **AI can already help run experiments, but it cannot yet replace judgment.** Some companies already use AI to design experiments and decide the order to run them in, and the boost is considerable. When it comes to decisions and judgment, though, AI still falls short. One company running a randomized Phase 2 trial worked closely with Anthropic in the months before database lock (the point after which trial data can no longer be changed).
2. **The hardest part of biotech is translating academic findings, and translation is at heart a reproducibility problem.** Most academic results cannot be carried straight into a drug program, which is the same problem as the failure to reproduce published results described earlier. One remedy is to work closely with academic founders who are willing to keep working on a finding. Their labs keep refining it long after the paper, which is closer to engineering, so what reaches the company is far more mature than what a typical lab hands over.
3. **A company founded this way starts much further along.** By the time the technology moves into a company founded by the professor who runs the lab or by one of the lab's students, it is still pre-IND (the IND is the application to start human trials), but it already has months of research and several rounds of lead optimization behind it. It is no longer just the version in the paper, and building the company becomes much easier.
4. **Lilly's TuneLab is a drugmaker getting into the data business itself.** In [September 2025 Lilly launched TuneLab, with models built on its own drug disposition (how a drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized and excreted), safety and preclinical data covering hundreds of thousands of molecules, backed by more than US$1 billion in research](https://investor.lilly.com/news-releases/news-release-details/lilly-launches-tunelab-platform-give-biotechnology-companies). Biotechs can use the models, and in return they [contribute their own data to improve them through federated learning](https://www.bio-itworld.com/news/2026/06/30/anthropic-launches-claude-science--illumina's-spatial-insights-tool--wellcome-sanger-institute--google-deepmind-ai-consortium), which trains a model on each party's data without moving or exposing it. Distribution has been quick: TuneLab is already plugged into [Benchling, whose customers include more than 1,300 biotechs](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/benchling-and-lilly-tunelab-partner-to-democratize-access-to-ai-models-for-scientists-everywhere-302655983.html), and into [CDD Vault](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/collaborative-drug-discovery-partners-with-lilly-tunelab-to-make-lilly-aiml-models-available-in-cdd-vault-302777087.html), while Charles River (CRL) provides testing for participating companies. TuneLab itself also [sits under Lilly's Catalyze360 program, right next to Lilly's venture arm](https://www.biopharmatrend.com/post/1363-lilly-offers-biotechs-access-to-ai-models-trained-on-1b-in-proprietary-drug-discovery-data). So Lilly keeps its own data, benefits from everyone else's without ever seeing it, and gets an early view of which biotechs are worth investing in or buying. That last part may be worth more than the models themselves.
5. **TuneLab still works on the cheap half.** By its own account, the models help participating companies weed out candidate molecules faster. That is useful, and it is graded by real experiments, but it falls within the nine percent before the first human dose and does not touch the question of whether a target matters in people. There are two further risks. A biotech feeding its data to a company that may well end up acquiring it will sooner or later hold some of that data back. And there is no public, pre-registered evidence yet on how much federated learning gains across labs whose experimental methods differ. There is also a side effect: a drugmaker that opens models trained on a billion dollars of its own data to biotechs, asking for data instead of money, puts direct pricing pressure on companies that sell property-prediction models. Frontier model companies such as Anthropic complement TuneLab: the language model runs the workflow and the property model scores the molecules, but neither makes the decisions that cost the most money.
6. **Johnson & Johnson shows what it looks like when the buyer sets the test.** Investors now say they want companies that can run their own design, make and test loop: companies that build AI into their own systems and, above all, generate their own data. Companies that build foundation models on public datasets are everywhere and cannot set themselves apart. In October 2025, J&J (JNJ) launched [a challenge in computational antibody design](https://cttc.co/node/624). According to people in the industry, each of the dozen or so antibody design companies taking part designed about 100 sequences for each of three targets, J&J is testing them in its own labs, and the winners will get the chance to work with J&J. The result is that J&J now holds head-to-head data on a dozen or so of the world's leading AI protein design companies, on the same targets. The companies have never had a benchmark like this, and all of them want the data, which J&J keeps.
7. **Data has to be distinctive, not just big.** At a recent panel of AI biotech founders, a few practical points came up. First, a model that understands DNA does not understand disease. The chain from DNA to RNA, proteins, cells and finally patient outcomes is long, and the data that exists is not enough to predict it end to end, so it is more practical to model each layer and then connect them. In the same way, understanding the variants that cause disease is not the same as understanding the disease. Second, an unusual biological phenotype can teach more than a pile of samples. One company, for example, studies how the hearts of hibernating animals repair themselves after injury, looking for new leads in heart failure. Third, the goal for a model is to predict how people respond to treatment, and predicting sequences better is only a step toward that. Fourth, drugmakers may come to want drug assets more than pure technology platforms. AI should also explore chemistry that traditional methods cannot reach, against targets that already have clinical validation, instead of squeezing more efficiency out of known chemistry. Finally, some think the wave of protein language models has matured and the next opportunity may lie in small molecules, but in the end the value of any model or dataset has to be proven by commercial results.

</details>

## Who is the hero, and who is the gimmick

There is one test running through all of this: who grades the results? A hero is graded from outside, by patients in a clinical trial, by a buyer who pays cash at signing, or by a wet lab that actually makes and tests the molecule. A gimmick grades itself, with a headline deal value, a pipeline slide in a business plan, an AI-discovered label, or a benchmark of its own choosing. By this test, what has worked in AI drug discovery is physics and chemistry computation aimed at specific problems and checked in the lab, and the value lies with the people and companies that pay for the trials and decide when to stop. Much of what has been sold under the name of AI drug discovery is just a label hung on the outside of that work.

Every hero in the table is graded by a clinical trial, a paying buyer or a lab, and every gimmick grades itself.

How do you spot a real hero? Look at just two things: real substance (data and molecules tested in a lab) and real money (cash paid at signing, or someone paying for the trial themselves). Ignore everything else.

## 钱花在临床，不花在发现

一个药物项目的钱，几乎全是在还没有一分钱收入时花掉的；而药一旦获批，就能赚很多年。专利从申请日起算有二十年，获批后通常还能独占市场十到十五年。每片药的生产成本接近于零，医保和患者也很少因为价格不用一个药。项目只要没走到获批，就一分钱也赚不到。想向药企卖东西，先得问清楚自己的产品落在项目成本的哪一段。这笔成本大部分花在临床，也就是人体试验上。临床试验分三期：I 期在一小群人身上测安全性，II 期在更多人身上测药有没有效，III 期则是支撑获批的大规模试验。

{% include posts/cheap-half/fig-1-zh.html %}

对一个走到首次进入人体的项目来说，此前的全部工作大约要花 1,560 万美元（按 2026 年美元计，下同），包括靶点工作、筛选（从大量化合物里找出能和靶点结合的那些）、先导优化（把一个有苗头的分子改造成能在人身上测试的分子），以及支持临床申请的动物实验。单是 III 期就要 1.18 亿美元，是前者的七倍多。

现在假设有一台完美的发现引擎：免费、即时，给它任何靶点，它都能返回现存最好的分子。即便如此，在一个约 1.74 亿美元的项目里（把图中各期加起来），它最多也只能省下 1,560 万美元。确实有些项目死在化学上，比如出现一个没人预料到的代谢产物（药在体内分解出来的东西），或者一个没人查过的心脏副作用。可这里已经假定这台引擎在化学上完美无缺，它仍然碰不到让大多数项目失败的那件事：靶点在人身上原来并不重要。算下来，这台引擎最多省下大约百分之九的成本，风险几乎没变。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>这张图没算进去的东西</summary>

1. **大部分钱花在失败的项目上。** [图后那份报告](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/ce102c41a7626cd885f7c88a9f2c4807/drug-development.pdf)估计，单个在研药物的平均现金支出（实际花出去的钱）约为 2.29 亿美元；把失败的项目和十年等待的资金成本一并算上，约为 11.6 亿美元。大多数项目会半路失败，获批的药要替它们把钱赚回来。
2. **大部分获批的药成不了重磅药。** 重磅药（blockbuster）指年销售额超过 10 亿美元的药，像 Keytruda 那样一年卖到 100 亿美元以上的就更少了。2014 到 2023 年 FDA 批准的 487 个药里，[Evaluate 预测其中 193 个（约 39%）的峰值年销售额能超过 10 亿美元，超过 100 亿美元的只有 17 个](https://content.evaluate.com/story/making-of-a-blockbuster/page/3)。
3. **前端便宜了确实有用，但钱只有出钱做临床的一方能收。** 前端指的是临床之前的发现阶段。在成功率不变的情况下，多几个候选分子就意味着多几个获批，所以更便宜的前端是值钱的。可失败集中在临床，每多一个候选分子，都要多花一份临床的钱，收益也归出这笔钱的一方。一台能让候选分子数量翻倍的发现引擎，如果没人出钱做试验，开发它的人一分钱也收不到。
4. **绝对数偏低，比例不变。** 这个模型的绝对数低于常被引用的大药厂口径。[DiMasi 等人](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2016.01.012)估计，单个在研药物的 III 期现金支出约为 3.64 亿美元（原文是 2013 年美元的 2.55 亿，这里换算成 2026 年美元）。[德勤最新的统计](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-shared/docs/industries/life-sciences-health-care/2026/measuring-the-return-from-pharmaceutical-innovation.pdf)（2025 年数据）显示，全球前 20 大药企把一个药从发现做到上市，平均要花 26.7 亿美元，失败项目的花费也摊在里面。各家估计的绝对数不尽相同，但在每一家的估计里，III 期都是最贵的一期，而且贵得多。
5. **成本占比小，分到的钱不一定也小。** 一项投入哪怕只占项目支出的百分之九，只要足够稀缺，就完全可能拿走远超百分之九的收入，软件生意天天都在这么做。可制药业的发现环节做不到，一个原因是买家太少。两个 III 期按 2026 年美元算要 2.4 亿到 7 亿多美元。能自己出这笔钱、同时做两个 III 期的，除了二十来家大药厂，大概只有一百家上下的 biotech：[美欧上市 biotech 里年收入超过 5 亿美元的有 72 家](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-gl/campaigns/life-sciences-securing-value-data-driven-platforms/documents/ey-gl-beyond-borders-biotech-report-06-2026.pdf)，再加上一些账上现金很多的临床阶段公司。而[做药的公司有将近七千家](https://www.bio.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/The%20State%20of%20Emerging%20Biotech%20Companies%20-%20Investment%2C%20Deal%2C%20and%20Pipeline%20Trends.pdf)。这些买家都养着自己的化学团队，谁也不缺分子。

</details>

## 第二个药常常赢

专利是一笔交换：公司拿到垄断权，代价是公开分子。公司接着去做临床，而临床会告诉所有旁观者，这个靶点靠不靠得住。所以在一类药（作用于同一个靶点的药算一类）里，第一家公司付了发现的钱、扛了靶点的风险，最后却把答案交给了竞争对手。第二家公司则可以从三样现成的东西出发：已经验证的靶点（试验已经表明它确实要紧）、公开的分子结构，以及披露过的临床终点（试验用来判断药有没有效的指标），再做出一个更好的药。

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-followers-zh.html %}

每一行里，后进入这一类的药要么成了更大的产品，要么在头对头试验（两个药直接对比的试验）中胜过了第一个药，而发现的钱都是第一家公司付的。

把这一节和上一节合起来看，任何卖“发现”的人都不会好受。如果第二个药常常赢，那么找新靶点就只是一笔成本，最好让别人的股东来掏。大药厂靠的是项目组合决策（决定把钱押在哪些项目上，比如跟进哪个已验证的靶点）和把临床做好，不靠发现。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>这张表里的幸存者偏差</summary>

1. **表里的赢家，都是行业里最大的产品。** 阿托伐他汀是第五个他汀（一类降胆固醇药），自家公司的高管差点把它停掉，理由是市场已经被别人占了。阿达木单抗是第三个抗 TNF（一种炎症信号）药，后来成了史上最畅销的药。泽布替尼是第三个 BTK 抑制剂（抑制剂指能压住某个酶活性的药），[在头对头试验里胜过了第一个](https://www.pubmed.gov/36395435/)。后文还会讲到的 zasocitinib，在第一个 TYK2 抑制剂获批四年后才递交上市申请，却在头对头试验里赢了它。
2. **决定胜负的是分子，收钱的却是做临床的公司。** 在一个已验证的类别里，分子是主要差别，这也是化学不会消失的原因。但每一行里胜出的分子，都出自赢家本来就有的化学团队，利润也归做赢了临床的那家公司。
3. **这张表只收录了后来真出了第二个药的类别。** 大多数靶点等不到第二个药：第一个药失败了，这个靶点也就没人再碰。而快速跟随者（fast follower）无药可跟的那些类别，没人会另做一张表去列。所以这条规律有前提：靶点靠得住，晚到的才占便宜。而靶点靠不靠得住，通常是别人花了十年和十亿美元才弄清楚的。
4. **跟随是大公司的策略。** 前提是你出得起要跟进的那个临床，之后还得有工厂和销售队伍去赢下市场。在减重药上，诺和诺德（NVO）的司美格鲁肽先上市，却[在 FDA 的短缺药品清单上待了 1,000 多天，先发优势因此打了折扣](https://www.biospace.com/business/obesity-leaders-dig-manufacturing-moats-to-defend-injectable-glp-1-empires)。礼来（LLY）的替尔泊肽在清单上少待了大约 300 天。此后诺和诺德的份额逐渐被礼来蚕食，供应可能是原因之一。

</details>

## 中国卖分子，能不能获批仍由买方说了算

如果药要过了临床才值钱，那么稀缺的就是把一个分子送过 FDA 的能力：撑得起 III 期的现金、和监管机构打过交道的记录，以及 FDA 愿意接受的数据。三样齐备的公司拿走大部分利润；三样都没有的公司只能当资产（也就是候选药物）供应商，便宜地做出分子，再卖给能把它推到获批的公司。

中国药企眼下就在大规模这么做。2025 年，它们签下 [157 笔对外授权（out-licensing，即出售一个候选药物的权益），公布总额 1,357 亿美元；2024 年则是 94 笔、519 亿美元](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-01-14/chinese-drugmakers-strike-record-136-billion-in-cross-border-licensing-deals-102403540.html)。自 2022 年以来，签约时平均实付的现金已经涨到了三倍多。不过有两件事没变：公布总额大多是里程碑付款（只有后续试验成功或销售达标才付的钱），而这些钱大部分永远不会付出来；决定这个分子有没有机会见到患者的，仍然是买方。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>交易数字背后</summary>

1. **价格涨了，交易还是老样子。** 截至 2026 年 10 月 8 日，当年对外授权的累计公布总额[已达 1,394 亿美元](https://www.nextmsc.com/news/chinas-growing-role-in-the-biotechnology-market)，超过了 2025 年全年。前三季度的首付款（upfront，签约时就支付的现金）为 80 亿美元，而 2025 年全年是 72 亿美元。按 Evaluate 的统计，[中国资产授权交易的平均首付款从 2022 年的 5,200 万美元涨到了 2026 年初的 1.72 亿美元](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/analyst-china-no-longer-bargain-basement-biotech-acquisitions)。这门生意是这样运作的：一家公司以五千万美元（和 2022 年的平均首付款差不多）把一个临床前（preclinical，还没进过人体）的分子卖掉，此后这个分子每转手一次，涨出来的钱都归下一个买家。2026 年 1 月 30 日，阿斯利康（AZN）[同意向石药（1093.HK）支付 12 亿美元现金首付款](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/astrazeneca-returns-chinas-cspc-47b-obesity-deal)，买下八个减重项目（每月注射一次）在中国以外的权益，而这些项目都还没走完 I 期。按五千万一个算，八个分子也就四亿美元上下，只有石药拿到的三分之一。辉瑞（PFE）则[同意为三生制药（1530.HK）的一个双特异性抗体（bispecific antibody，能同时结合两个靶点的抗体药）支付 12.5 亿美元首付款](https://www.biospace.com/business/pfizers-china-deal-worth-up-to-6b-eclipses-peers-in-a-frenzied-market)，这个抗体当时[已在中国开展好几项临床试验](https://www.pfizer.com/news/press-release/press-release-detail/pfizer-enters-exclusive-licensing-agreement-3sbio)。
2. **还剩多少折价，分析师看法不一。** 按 Clarivate（CLVT）的统计，[2025 年上半年全球对外授权交易中有 32% 来自中国公司，2020 年只有 5%；但中国资产的首付款仍比西方同类资产低 60% 到 70%，交易总额小 40% 到 50%](https://pharmasource.global/content/chart/china-now-sources-32-of-global-biopharma-out-licensing-deals-up-from-5-in-2020/)。而 2026 年初，Evaluate 的分析师[认为中国公司的首付款正在向其他地区的交易看齐](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/analyst-china-no-longer-bargain-basement-biotech-acquisitions)。不管哪边说得对，卖方的钱主要是签约那一笔，药上市以后赚的归买方。
3. **单一国家的数据是弱点。** 信迪利单抗是信达生物（1801.HK）的 PD-1 抑制剂（一种肿瘤免疫治疗药），由礼来引进，只在中国做过试验。2022 年 2 月，FDA 肿瘤药物顾问委员会[以 14 比 1 投票](https://cancerletter.com/regulatory-news/20220211_1/)认为，FDA 在做决定之前，应先要求补做试验，证明结果适用于美国患者。这个委员会只是顾问性质，投票问的也是要不要补试验，而不是能不能批；但 FDA 自己的简报文件列了三个条件，说明只凭国外试验数据获批要满足什么，并认定那项试验不满足。FDA 随后拒绝了这项申请。[索凡替尼即便附带了一项美国桥接试验（为证明结果适用于美国患者而补做的小型试验），也因类似理由被拒](https://pharmaphorum.com/market-access-2/fdas-treatment-of-china-developed-drugs-spurs-demands/)。2026 年 5 月，FDA 细胞和基因治疗审评部门的代理负责人[表示，把首批人体试验放到美国以外去做，短期内也许有好处，长远看却可能适得其反](https://friendsofcancerresearch.org/news/pink-sheet-first-in-china-trials-may-be-counterproductive-for-sponsors-us-fda-cber-official-says/)。
4. **这条规矩并非绝对。** 特瑞普利单抗[于 2023 年 10 月 27 日获批](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/resources-information-approved-drugs/fda-approves-toripalimab-tpzi-nasopharyngeal-carcinoma)用于鼻咽癌，依据是一项单区域的 III 期和一项单国家的 II 期，而且此前还因生产问题被拒过。但多区域临床试验如今已是默认要求，这笔成本要么由卖方承担，要么体现为价格上的折扣。
5. **眼下的检验是依沃西单抗。** 这是康方生物（9926.HK）的双特异性抗体，同时针对 PD-1 和 VEGF（血管内皮生长因子，肿瘤靠它长出新血管），美国、欧洲和其他一些市场的权益授权给了 Summit Therapeutics（SMMT）。[康方已经报告了三项只在中国开展的肺癌 III 期试验的阳性结果，其中 HARMONi-A 显示出统计学上显著（statistically significant，即不太可能是偶然造成的）的生存获益。但 Summit 向 FDA 申报时依据的是 HARMONi，一项在多个地区开展的 III 期](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1599298/000159929826000006/a2026_prx0129xfdablaacce.htm)，FDA 的决定日期是 2026 年 11 月 14 日。FDA 曾[明确要求总生存期（overall survival，即患者活了多久）出现统计学上显著的获益，而 HARMONi 没有做到](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/summit-therapeutics-updates-ivonescimab-survival-data-ahead-fda-decision-date)。死亡风险比（hazard ratio）比较的是两组患者的死亡风险：1.0 表示没有差别，数值越低，说明新药越好。在 HARMONi 的 165 名北美和欧洲患者里，2025 年 4 月主分析时这个数字是 0.98，而整个试验是 0.79。这些患者入组比预期慢，当时随访时间的中位数只有 9.2 个月。在 2026 年 7 月公布的更新里，他们的风险比是 0.76，与整个试验此前公布过的 0.78 和 0.79 相差无几。FDA 在 11 月 14 日要判断的是：没有达到统计学显著的生存结果够不够用，结果又是否适用于美国患者。

</details>

## 管线这道题，没有好答案

AI 制药公司，以及越来越多的 AI for science 公司，迟早都要回答一个问题：要不要做自己的管线（pipeline，指公司自己推进到临床的一组候选药物）？无非做或不做，两个答案都不好。回答不做，公司就是一家卖模型的软件公司，行业里管这叫平台。它可以在 benchmark（用来比较模型的标准测试）上发结果，甚至拿第一，这足以撑到第二轮融资前后。到那时投资人会问收入从哪来，答案只有几份研究合同，而投资人当初是按软件公司给它估值的。

回答做，公司就成了一家 biotech。Biotech 的估值看管线，管线需要临床的钱，这时往平台上多投一块钱，撑起估值的候选药物就少拿一块钱。平台和资产争预算，每次都是资产赢，因为候选药物有一个明确的日子（临床出结果的那天），平台却没有。还有第三个答案：早早把分子卖掉来养平台。这能让公司活下来，但只能当资产供应商。薛定谔（SDGR）、Recursion（RXRX）和 Isomorphic Labs 都已经公开回答过这个问题。

合并时承诺了约十个临床数据读出（readout，即试验结果揭晓）；十个月之内，Recursion 就停掉或暂停了三个中期项目，还裁掉了五分之一的员工。

{% include posts/cheap-half/fig-2-zh.html %}

买方与 Alphabet（GOOGL）旗下那家药物发现公司合作，只付了 8,250 万美元现金；买一个已走完 IIb 期（II 期中规模更大、更靠后的阶段）的分子，却付了 40 亿美元现金。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>证据，一家一家看</summary>

1. **薛定谔占尽优势，最后也做起了管线。** 它成立于 1990 年，是最有名的计算化学软件公司，后文要讲的 zasocitinib 用的就是它的软件。几十年来它的回答都是“不做”，后来改成了“做”，把三个全资的肿瘤项目推进了临床，其中一个[在 2025 年 8 月因两名患者死亡而终止](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/schrodinger-abandons-cdc7-inhibitor-after-therapy-linked-2-patient-deaths)。到 2025 年年中，它已经在[为其中领头的项目寻找战略选项](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/schrodinger-reports-second-quarter-2025-financial-results)，实际上就是找别人来付临床的钱。2025 年它[报告收入 2.56 亿美元，经营亏损 1.67 亿美元](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490978/000149097826000010/sdgr-20251231.htm)；靠 6,460 万美元的其他收益（其中约四分之三是股权投资的账面增值），净亏损才收窄到 1.03 亿美元。
2. **不做管线的 AI 公司，做的其实是高科技 CRO。** CRO 是医药外包公司。不少 AI 公司干的活和它差不多：用算法帮药企在大量方案里筛一遍，告诉对方哪几个更靠谱，说到底是帮客户省时间。这种生意按项目收钱，做一单领一单，没法像软件那样一次开发、反复卖。等药走到临床、生产和销售，主动权就全在买家手里：买家有钱、有工厂、有渠道，付一笔钱把专利买断或者拿个授权，后面这个药赚多少，都和卖方无关。想拿销售分成或里程碑付款，就得回答一个问题：买家凭什么把这块蛋糕分给你？如果买家自己组一支 AI 团队也能做，或者随时能换一家供应商，答案就是不会分。
3. **用不了几个预算周期，管线就会吃掉平台的全部预算。** 单个项目的一次小规模生产要四千万人民币上下，就像芯片流片（把设计好的芯片送到工厂试产）：钱投下去，然后等，最后才知道结果。一家公司没法用同一份预算，既养这件事，又养一个研究平台。再过两三个预算周期，平台就成了公司内部的服务组，公司本身则成了一家中型 biotech：六个项目，现金只够撑几个月。这样的公司比比皆是，换个更好的模型也改变不了。
4. **第三个答案会把 AI 公司变成中国药企那样的资产供应商。** 分子照样做，但早早卖掉，用卖分子的钱养平台，也就是常说的以药养研。英矽智能（3696.HK）用自家的生成式 AI 平台发现了抗癌分子 ISM3091，[2023 年把它授权给 Exelixis（EXEL），拿到 8,000 万美元首付款](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/artificial-intelligence/stockwatch-insilico-ceo-breaks-down-exelixis-deal/)，当时这个分子刚进入 I 期一个月。英矽的首席执行官说，原因之一是公司管线太挤，31 个项目对应 29 个靶点。中国药企走的是这条路的一个变体，叫 NewCo：[投资人在中国境外新设一家公司，由它拿到资产权益并出钱做临床](https://www.pharmaceutical-technology.com/features/newco-licensing-models-are-tipping-deals-in-favour-of-chinas-biotechs/)。原研公司和投资人共同持股，原研公司照样收首付款和分成（royalty，从将来的销售额里抽取的比例）。恒瑞医药（600276.SH、1276.HK）的减重药 HRS9531（和司美格鲁肽、替尔泊肽同属一类）就是这样授权给 NewCo 公司 Kailera Therapeutics 的。早早卖掉能让平台转下去，代价是只能拿早期的价格，药以后赚多少都和自己无关。
5. **Recursion 和 Exscientia 先合并，再砍项目、裁员。** 它们是西方最显眼的两家平台公司，[2024 年合并](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/after-tough-year-exscientia-folds-recursion-create-ai-super-power)时承诺在 18 个月内交出约十个临床数据读出。三个月后开始裁员。到第九个月，[两个中期项目被停，第三个被暂停](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/recursion-pipeline-cuts-first-quarter-earnings/747119/)；到第十个月，五分之一的员工离开，研发收缩到只剩肿瘤和罕见病。
6. **两笔平台交易加起来，只拿到一笔分子交易的五十分之一。** Isomorphic Labs 是 Alphabet 旗下的药物发现公司，以 AlphaFold（预测蛋白质结构的模型）为基础，由一位诺奖得主掌舵。2024 年 1 月，它签下[最初的两份药厂合作](https://pharmaphorum.com/news/isomorphic-signs-lilly-novartis-3bn-ai-drug-hunt)，公布总额约 30 亿美元，其中现金 8,250 万美元。2022 年 12 月，武田（TAK）以 40 亿美元保底现金、外加最多 20 亿美元的销售里程碑，[买下 Nimbus 的 TYK2 子公司](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/nimbus-used-ai-help-develop-125737581.html)，买的是一个分子，当时[已经走完银屑病的 IIb 期](https://www.schrodinger.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/23_530_TYK2-Case-Study_Mkt_R7-4_Digital.pdf)。Nimbus 同样是一家做计算药物设计的公司。现金差了大约五十倍，最明显的区别是 Nimbus 手里有分子。差距恐怕主要也不在临床数据：看阿斯利康和石药那根柱子，石药的领头项目当时也还没进过人体，照样拿到 12 亿美元现金，因为后面跟着八个具体的分子。
7. **Isomorphic 也没绕开这道题。** 平台靠投资人的钱活着，买家付的现金撑不起它。2026 年 5 月，Isomorphic [由 Thrive Capital 领投，融资 21 亿美元](https://www.biospace.com/business/ai-fueled-isomorphic-bags-2-1b-the-second-largest-biotech-round-ever)，累计约 27 亿美元，而它没有披露过任何一个管线分子，也没有任何东西进入临床。按公司自己的说法，这笔钱用来继续开发平台，再借平台加快扩充管线、把项目推进临床。钱最多的发现平台，融了 21 亿美元，是为了把自己变成一家 biotech。
8. **大部分初创公司说不出自己的分子，上市的几家底下是传统药企。** 问一家 AI 制药初创公司：有没有哪个分子，是你们的平台做出来、换一支称职的人类团队就做不出来的？大部分举不出来。BP（商业计划书）里有一页管线，管线底下是常规化学。上市的那几家，则是一个名字下的两个组织：前台挂着 AI 的牌子负责融资，后台是一个传统药物研发组织在干活。年景一紧，AI 组往往先挨刀，不过上面那张 Recursion 的表看不出哪个组先被砍，只看得出合并后的公司砍了项目、裁掉五分之一的人，合并前的两家都有人被裁。
9. **在香港上市的两家知名 AI 制药公司，对管线问题给出了相反的答案。** 下文称它们为 A 公司和 B 公司。A 公司基本上答的是不做。它卖药物发现服务、实验室机器人和它所谓的 AI for Science 解决方案，客户除了药企，还有新材料和消费健康行业。它 [2025 年通过配售新股融资超过 7.5 亿美元](https://conventuslaw.com/?p=129124)，当年[首次实现全年盈利，利润 1.346 亿元人民币，收入 8.026 亿元](https://xtalpi-ir-umb.azurewebsites.net/media/tsnngqsl/2026032501035.pdf)。但据[它自己的公告](https://www.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2026/0303/2026030301645.pdf)，机器人和药物发现这两块核心业务仍在亏损，只是亏得比上一年少。利润主要来自两处。一是它持有的金融资产升值了至少 5 亿元。二是少了一笔账面亏损：2024 年它曾因上市前发行的优先股记了一大笔亏损，这些优先股上市时已转为普通股，2025 年就不再有这笔亏损。撇开这两项，A 公司就是一家服务公司，外加一大笔投资组合，收入来自客户。B 公司答的是做。它 [2025 年底上市，并称这是当年香港最大的一笔生物科技融资](https://insilico.com/news/ohz9ozx0t1-insilico-medicine-announces-2025-annual)。2025 年它[收入 5,620 万美元，经调整亏损 4,380 万美元，研发投入约为收入的 145%](https://www.chinadailyhk.com/upload/main/pdf/2026/04/17/6a6f6d898d1ebab8985ef1fc3898c6d3.pdf)。按它自己的说法，它最新推出的八个心血管和代谢疾病分子，[针对的是 GLP-1R（司美格鲁肽的靶点）、Lp(a)（血液里一种和心脏病相关的颗粒）这类已经确证、把握很高的靶点，再加上几个较新的靶点，要在化学上和别人拉开差距](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1105110)。这就是第二个药的打法，没什么不对。它的领头项目是个例外：靶点是新的，而且是它自己的平台找出来的。它预计在 2026 年上半年[实现盈利，主要靠对外授权和合作研发收入](https://www1.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2026/0709/2026070900039.pdf)，也就是上文说的第三个答案。
10. **为什么挂 AI 的牌子有用，又为什么很少有公司肯出钱做研究。** AI 的牌子之所以有用，是因为它能降低融资成本：只要做成一个药，投资人就会把它当作证据，相信背后的平台还能做出更多药。大多数公司不愿为研究本身花钱，是因为可以搭便车。第一家公司在“让模型管用”这件事上学到的东西，别人看一眼它的结果就能免费学到，所以理性的做法是捂着现金，等别人先试，跟第二个药一样。

## 这已经是第三次了

有人拿一项让发现阶段便宜很多的技术，对这个行业说“药物发现已经解决了”。这种事以前发生过两次：1990 年代的基因组测序，和 2010 年代 IBM（IBM）的 Watson。每一次，都有一家公司的估值押在“前端就是整门生意”上，要撑住这个估值，就得真做出一个药。Human Genome Sciences 为此变成了一家 biotech，结果撞上了临床的成本和靶点的风险；IBM 的医疗部门最后以远低于投入的价格被卖掉。两次都是技术留下来当工具，被原本就在这个行业里的公司按工具的价格买走，估值没了。这一波名气最大的 BenevolentAI 已经走了一遍；这次钱更多，故事和生意都没变。

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-waves-zh.html %}

每一行里，技术都还在用，靠它估值的公司却被卖掉、收缩或退市。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>每一波留下了什么</summary>

1. **Human Genome Sciences 不是炒作，科学也没错。** 基因组、专利和药都是真的：贝利尤单抗是五十年来第一个新的狼疮药。只不过它花了二十年才做出一个药，因为在 1990 年代的药物发现里，测序就是便宜的那一段，就像现在的分子设计。这家给基因申请专利的公司，最后被一家能做临床的公司买走，原来的管理层十五天之内全部走人。
2. **BenevolentAI 把同一条路走得更快。** 它自称“生物科学的机器大脑”，按二十亿美元估值融资，并通过欧洲最大的一笔 SPAC（借壳上市的一种方式）交易上市。投资人问药在哪儿，它就建了管线；管线把现金烧光了，它又砍掉管线。它一轮接一轮地裁员，宣布回头去卖平台，最后退市。整个过程用了十二年。
3. **技术全都留了下来。** 基因组学如今是整个行业做靶点验证（确认这个靶点在人身上真的和疾病有关）的基础，没有它，谁也不会立项。读文献的自然语言系统进了各大药厂。下一节讲的那个分子，就是用自由能计算设计出来的。
4. **它们没看清工具的钱归谁。** 做工具的人并不拥有工具找到的东西：Illumina（ILMN）卖测序仪，却不拥有用测序仪找到的药。如果买家自己也做得出这种工具（化学软件就是如此），做工具的人拿到的就更少，因为工具的售价不会高于买家自己做一件的成本。薛定谔卖 FEP 软件（free-energy perturbation，自由能扰动，也就是上一段说的自由能计算），可它从 zasocitinib 上分到的 [1.11 亿美元](https://ir.schrodinger.com/press-releases/news-details/2023/Schrdinger-Receives-111.3-Million-Distribution-from-Sale-of-Nimbuss-TYK2-Inhibitor-to-Takeda-02-14-2023/default.aspx)，来自它持有的 Nimbus 股份，而不是软件。在第一个临床读出之前，一个发现平台都只是一件工具，估值却是按药企给的。
5. **到目前为止，唯一找到的出路是把资产卖掉。** Human Genome Sciences 的办法是被收购，Nimbus 的办法是卖掉子公司。BenevolentAI 没做到，因为等它想卖的时候，手上已经没有任何人想要的资产了。
6. **生物科技股远远落后于科技股。** 按复权月收盘价算，从 2011 年底到 2026 年 10 月底，代表中小型生物科技股的 XBI 涨了约 632%，跟踪纳斯达克 100、以大型科技公司为主的 QQQ 涨了约 1,304%，两者相差近 700 个百分点。疫情以前两者差不多：2011 年底到 2019 年底，XBI 涨了约 343%，还略好于 QQQ 的 315%。差距几乎都是 2020 年以后拉开的，而这几年偏偏是 AI 进步最快、AI 制药融资最热的时候。指数涨跌有利率、并购、药价政策等很多原因，不能全算在 AI 头上，但至少看不出 AI 带动了整个生物科技行业起飞。

</details>

## 计算到底做出了什么

现在有两类证据。一类是总体数据：2024 年的一次统计，看的是以 AI 平台为核心的 biotech 做出的分子，凡是走完过某个临床阶段的都算在内。另一类是计算设计出的最强的单个分子。总体上看，AI 设计的分子通过 I 期的比例远高于历史水平，通过 II 期的比例则和历史水平相当。I 期问的是工程问题：分子在目标剂量下安不安全，在人体里的表现是否符合化学上的预测。II 期问的是科学问题：这个靶点在这种病里到底重不重要。最强的单个分子 zasocitinib 用计算解决了一个困难的化学问题，但它面对的是一个别人已经验证过的靶点。到目前为止，计算改进的那一段，本来就不是项目主要失败的地方。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>两类证据背后的数字</summary>

1. **总体数据。** 2024 年，咨询公司 BCG 的一个团队[把 AI 原生 biotech 所有走完过某个临床阶段的分子梳理了一遍](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drudis.2024.104009)。走完 I 期的 24 个分子里有 21 个通过，接近九成（报告写的是 80% 到 90%），而历史水平是 40% 到 65%。走完 II 期的 10 个分子里有 4 个通过，约 40%，历史水平是 30% 到 40%。作者自己的解读是，AI 很擅长做出具备成药性质（吸收、代谢、安全性等做成药所需的性质）的分子。按两个区间的中间值比较，AI 设计的分子在工程问题上的通过率比历史水平高出大约二十到四十个百分点；在科学问题上看上去和历史水平一样，但只有十个分子，少到说明不了任何事。作者也把“靶点已经充分验证”列为 I 期通过率高的可能原因之一，不过他们认为这解释不了全部。
2. **筛选成功率翻倍，对买家值多少钱。** 这笔账很简单：如果买家自己的筛选成功率是 10%，供应商的是 20%，那他筛两次就行了。他只需要一个命中（筛出一个能结合靶点的化合物），筛选又做得起，所以成功率翻倍只是让他的工作量减半。这就像外卖平台的优惠券：手头紧的人会用，有钱的人看都不看。这份价值是真的，只是远没有 BP 里写的那么高。例外有两个。一是时间：如果专利申请正等着筛选结果，快一点就值钱。二是根本没法筛的靶点，只有在这种情况下，模型才算打开了新的可能。模型拿得出手的，只能是把零变成非零，一成变两成不算数，而这一步目前还没人做到。
3. **最强的个案：zasocitinib。** 2016 年，Nimbus 和薛定谔的联合团队着手做一个有选择性的 TYK2 抑制剂。TYK2 属于 JAK 酶家族，而 TYK2、JAK1、JAK2、JAK3 的结合位点几乎一模一样，所以选择性（只打中 TYK2、不碰其他几个）就是全部难点。他们先从 JH1 入手，也就是催化结构域（酶里真正干活的部分），用自由能计算[给大约四千个设计打分，挑出 46 个合成，其中九个达到项目标准](https://www.drugpatentwatch.com/blog/artificial-intelligence-in-drug-discovery-what-is-realistic-what-are-illusions-part-1-ways-to-make-an-impact-and-why-we-are-not-there-yet/)。随后他们转向远离催化结构域的调控结构域 JH2，[针对一组蛋白结构模型，在计算机上筛选了 240 万个市售化合物](https://www.schrodinger.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/23_530_TYK2-Case-Study_Mkt_R7-4_Digital.pdf)，找到一种新的起始分子，再把它优化成 zasocitinib。正是因为结合的是调控结构域而不是催化结构域，选择性才做得出来：[据武田介绍](https://www.takeda.com/newsroom/newsreleases/2026/fda-priority-review-zasocitinib-psoriasis/)，这个分子对其他 JAK 酶的选择性在百万倍以上。这道药物化学题又难又贵，是计算解出来的。
4. **细看下来，没宣传的那么神。** 验证靶点、找到别构口袋（allosteric pocket，远离酶活性位点的一个结合位点）的是百时美施贵宝（BMY）。Nimbus 是后来者，面对的是一个风险已经被别人排除的靶点，做的又是第二个药。它用的是物理计算，也就是前面说的 FEP，而不是在化学文本上训练出来的生成模型。Nimbus 自己的研发总裁[明确说过](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/nimbus-used-ai-help-develop-125737581.html)，对一个早在 2020 年就已确定的化合物，他不会用“第一个 AI 获批药”这种说法。而提供那套软件的公司是薛定谔，如今它除了卖软件，也在做自己的管线。管用的这一套需要三样东西：别人已经验证过的靶点，窄到能当物理问题来解的题目，以及每一轮都合成得起、测得起几十个分子的湿实验室（动手做合成和测试的实验室）。
5. **FEP 算什么，不算什么。** FEP 估算的是两个相似分子与同一个蛋白结构的结合自由能之差：模拟其中一个分子逐步变成另一个，一路把水、蛋白和配体（也就是候选分子）的能量都算进去。做得好的时候（采样充分、结构可靠），精度大约在每摩尔 1 千卡，相当于亲和力（结合的紧密程度）差五倍左右，足以给相似的分子排序、决定下一轮做什么。它不会告诉你靶点选得对不对。它也不预测化合物会不会一小时就被代谢掉、会不会阻断 hERG 通道（一种心脏离子通道，被阻断会带来心律失常风险）、会不会在肝脏里蓄积、在小鼠身上有没有效。它默认你已经知道分子在口袋里是怎么待着的；一旦化学改动很大、蛋白会动，或者结构本身就是错的，它很快就不准了。它只答一个很窄的问题，一轮轮设计、合成、测试里的其他问题，还得靠实验。

</details>

## 真正要紧的知识从来没有被写下来

问一位药物化学“老法师”在一轮先导优化里做什么，答案听起来很程式化：看活性数据（分子作用于靶点强弱的测试结果），挑下一批类似物（结构稍作改动的分子），送去合成，等三四个星期，再看一遍。可如果再追问一句，“老法师”和新手差在哪儿，答案就不再程式化了。

差别在几个判断上：数据里哪些是信号、哪些是噪声，哪些路以前试过又放弃了，下一批做哪几个分子，以及什么时候停。这些都不在文献里，因为文献记录的是成功的东西；而且已发表的结果本身也不够可靠，当不了训练数据。安进（AMGN）和拜耳（BAYN.DE）在把已发表的结果当作项目基础之前，先去重复了这些结果，大部分都没能完全对上。大模型发布时报告的科学 benchmark 也测不到这些判断：它们考的是知识问答和机器学习工程（MLE），下面细说。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>记录里缺了什么</summary>

1. **“老法师”到底在决定什么。** 他要判断一张构效关系表（记录分子结构改动和活性变化的表）里，哪个差异是信号、哪个是实验噪声：同一个化合物重测一次，结果就能差出两倍，所以两倍的改善什么也说明不了，而分辨这一点的能力哪里都没写。他记得某个骨架（分子的核心结构）在他上一家公司因为毒性被放弃过，而那次毒性从未发表。他要在合成预算之内，从一千个合理的类似物里挑出三十个，而且知道自己一个月只能看一次结果。他还要判断一个系列（围绕同一个骨架的一组分子）什么时候算是死了：在他做的所有事情里，这件事的财务后果最大，手上的证据却最少。
2. **失败不公开，公开的记录也不可靠。** 失败都留在公司内部数据库里，那是这个行业捂得最紧的资产，所以用公开记录训练出来的模型，学到的大多是成功的尝试。安进的血液与肿瘤团队花了几年时间，在把项目押上去之前，先去[重复了 53 篇里程碑式的癌症论文](https://doi.org/10.1038/483531a)，最后确认了六篇。[拜耳的一个团队复盘了自己的 67 个内部项目](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd3439-c1)，发现只有大约 20% 到 25% 的项目里，已发表数据和他们自己的结果完全吻合。第三项重复研究是 [Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology，它重复了 23 篇高影响力论文里的 50 个实验](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.71601)。对原文里的阳性结果，重复出来的效应量（effect size，实验测到的变化有多大）中位数小了 85%。在能评估的效应里，达到大多数成功标准的不到一半（46%）。原因不复杂：每篇论文都需要一个还没人占的基因，所以几乎随便拿哪个基因和“肿瘤”一起搜，都能搜到把两者联系起来的论文。所以大药厂内部研究部门有很大一部分活是过滤：项目押上去之前，先把已发表的结论重复一遍，扔掉站不住的。
3. **谁来出卷子。** 药物发现没法自己挑裁判。先是人体来判，然后是 FDA 来判。没有人能自己定义一个 benchmark，在上面赢了，就宣布问题已经解决，而计算机视觉和语言建模每天都在这么做。让外人来判很花钱，好处是骗不了自己太久。要训练上面说的那种判断力，你需要的数据很枯燥，也很具体：来自真实项目内部的当时的局面、做出的决定和后来的结果。每一个系列都要记下当时知道什么、挑了哪三十个化合物、回来的数据是什么、系列死在了哪里。失败要记（失败占了大多数），还要带时间戳，这样给模型打分时，只用当时能知道的信息，不掺进事后才知道的东西。这样的数据以碎片的形式散落在几十家公司内部，而且不会被汇总，因为失败记录本身就是竞争资产。这是制度问题，加算力解决不了。
4. **大模型发布时报告的科学 benchmark，就是 Kaggle 比赛。** Kaggle 比赛给所有人同一份固定数据和一个问题，标准答案攥在主办方手里。你提交，马上拿到分数，还可以再交。大模型发布时报告的科学 benchmark 大致有三类（见上表），每一类都有这几样：一份固定的数据、一个问题、一个早就写好的答案。[有一个常用的智能体 benchmark，干脆就是 75 场 Kaggle 比赛，用 Kaggle 自己的排行榜来打分](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/7e3767db483c942b883eb4f8cfb74e31-Abstract-Conference.html)。有些新测试用上了真实数据和真实的研究流程，这是进步，但每一道题仍然出自别人手里已有的结果。考高分只说明模型找得到别人已有的答案，药物项目要的是谁都还没有的答案。
5. **这个领域里的批评者也是这么说的。** 药物发现领域内部最常用的排行榜，也是同样的结构。一位在工业界做了多年化学信息学（用计算机处理化学数据的学科）的研究者认为，[用得最多的那几套排行榜缺陷多到无法据此得出结论](http://practicalcheminformatics.blogspot.com/2023/08/we-need-better-benchmarks-for-machine.html)。2025 年 12 月，一篇有 56 位作者的论文[认为，主流的科学 benchmark 测的是脱离语境的知识点，忽略了推动科学发现的几样东西：一步步的推理、提出假设和解读观察结果](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15567)。
6. **真正的 AI for science 成果长什么样。** 这种成果，是对一件还没人知道的事做出预测，再由别人事后检验。在药物发现里，这意味着在分子合成之前就预测它能结合，或者在临床试验之前就预测某个靶点要紧，然后由实验室或临床试验给这个预测打分。数学里已经有这样的结果：[有模型把 11 维空间中亲吻数（最多能有多少个同样大的球同时碰到一个球）的已知下界从 592 提高到了 593](https://spectrum.ieee.org/deepmind-alphaevolve)。这种排列此前没人找到过，而谁都可以核对。数学上的结果，别的数学家就能核对；药物发现上的结果，却只能在实验室或者患者身上核对，要花几个月甚至几年，还要花真金白银。所以大模型公司报的，都是当天就能出分的测试。

</details>

## 你们在自动化最便宜的那一段

现在说“AI 制药”，基本就是指一个提分子的模型。这项工作落在进入人体之前那百分之九的成本里，而项目通常不是死在这一段。它现存最好的版本需要一个别人已经验证过的靶点；资金最多的那个平台 Isomorphic，最初两份合作一共只拿到 8,250 万美元现金，而一个进过人体的分子卖了 40 亿美元。

按这个行业自己的经济学，花钱最多的决策是这些：跟进哪个已验证的靶点；要不要买进外部的候选药物、签约时付多少；临床怎么设计；还有最重要的一项：什么时候停，停一个系列还是停整个项目。这些决策都由少数几位资深人士在每周例会上做出，可从来没有人在答案揭晓之前，把他们当时知道什么、预计会发生什么写下来，好在结果出来后拿来对照。这些决策不常有，每次都很贵，事后也没人对过账。

{% include posts/cheap-half/table-scoreboard-zh.html %}

大家拿来当检验的那两个药，短期内都定不了胜负：设计 zasocitinib 的公司自己都不愿叫它第一个 AI 获批药；英矽智能的 rentosertib（治肺纤维化的药，也是最常被称作 AI 发现的那个分子），III 期试验完全在中国做，结果要到 2029 年 12 月才出来。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>缺失的记录与三个检验</summary>

1. **这些决策里，有一部分已经有人建过模、审计过。** 剂量由暴露与反应分析（分析药效如何随剂量和血药浓度变化）来定。阿斯利康公开发表过[一份成绩单，对比自己改革项目推进和止损决策前后的结果](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd.2017.244)：从被选为候选药物到完成 III 期的比例，从 4% 升到了 19%。但每次决策背后的理由，哪里都没有记录。这不怪做决策的人，他们的经验是真的，通常也比别的办法好。
2. **记录为什么不存在。** 医院里的临床决策最能说明这件事，项目组合会议（公司决定把钱投给哪些项目的会）也是一个道理。临床上，一个决定的依据是责任：患者出了事谁赔钱，谁就说了算；而拿来做决定的，是一个没人量化过的判断，比如这个肾有多差，这个血压算多高，这个患者的年龄该让公式调整多少。于是决定出来就是“别的中心用 200 的剂量，我们用 150”，理由没写下来。这是 street smart，不是 book smart，而且确实管用。项目组合会议的逻辑也一样：项目失败时谁担责，就由谁拍板；而一条写下来的预测，日后可能被拿来追究写它的人，所以它不会被写下来。可只要有人在结果出来之前把预测写下来，这种知识立刻就能被检验。
3. **这类决策很适合交给模型。** 它们罕见而且昂贵，所以每次少错一点都值很多钱，也没有人需要毫秒级的答案。决定本身已经记进了会议纪要，因为它们都是在有固定议程的会上做出的。没写下来的，是做决定时知道什么；要记下来，改一改会议纪要的格式就够了。
4. **难在怎么评判“停掉”的决定。** 你永远只能看到自己做了的那个决定的结果，被你停掉的系列永远不会有数据。所以事后复盘没法给一个“停掉”的决定打分。能打分的有两样：对继续推进的系列，在结果出来之前登记好的预测；以及模型和人意见不一致时，被采纳那一方的判断。所以要靠事先写好、标了日期的预测，光有数据库不行。
5. **没有人发表过这个检验。** 有一些公司在卖其中的碎片：临床结果预测、数字对照组（用历史数据代替真实的对照组患者）、项目组合分析。但没有一家发表过这样一份带时间戳的记录：在同一批决策上，把模型的判断和人的判断摆在一起，并且在数据读出之前就登记好。钱和人才却都涌向了分子生成器。
6. **两个检验案例定不了这件事。** Zasocitinib 的监管结果 2027 年第一季度揭晓，但设计它的公司自己都不叫它 AI 药。Rentosertib 的试验在中国的 47 个中心开展，要想在美国获批，还得先过前面说的单一国家数据这一关。能检验 AI 分子设计的只剩 rentosertib，结果要到 2029 年才出来。就算它成功了，回答的也只是预算里百分之九那一段的问题。
7. **2026 年的一篇观点文章得出了同样的结论。** [《Nature Reviews Drug Discovery》2026 年发表的一篇观点文章](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41573-026-01496-2)，作者里有提出 Eroom 定律的研究者（Eroom 定律说的是，每个新药的研发成本几十年来不降反升），文章认为 AI 对临床的影响“有限得令人失望”。AI 设计的药，照样卡在 II 期这一关；在研发各个环节做同样幅度的改进，提高临床成功率（尤其是 II 期）回报最大。照这个思路，一个模型值多少钱，大致等于它把临床成功率提高了多少，乘以它能用在多少项目上。一个分子可以和受体结合得很紧，在细胞实验里杀伤力很强，在小鼠身上也能缩小肿瘤，到了人身上却可能没效果，或者只对一小部分人有效。只有在 II 期试验里拿出统计学上显著的疗效，才算得上是药。而学术界追逐影响因子和经费，重要的负面结果很难发表；主打 AI 的公司又偏爱讲大故事。统计学家 John Tukey 说过：对正确的问题给出近似答案，远好过对错误的问题给出精确答案。
8. **什么会推翻这个结论。** 第一，一家平台公司在一个仍由它持有的资产上走到 III 期读出，而且在融资过程中没有把自己变成一家常规 biotech。那就说明管线困境可以绕开，这里描述的结构是错的。第二，一个没有任何临床资产的发现平台拿到五亿美元以上的首付款，这里说的是首付款，股权融资不算。那就说明买家开始为方法本身定价，上面那个五十比一就过时了。第三，有人从真实项目内部发表一份带时间戳的成绩单，显示在预算相同、失败也计入的前提下，模型在上面列出的那些决策上胜过了有经验的人。这是三件里最便宜的一件：一家本来就在开这些会的公司，完全可以在结果回来之前把每一次预测登记下来，项目结束时公布成绩单，不增加任何实验成本。可至今没人拿出来。

</details>

## 论文靠不住，可靠的数据在湿实验室里

现在很多 AI for Science 的做法，是让模型去读论文、专利和各种知识库，再从这些文字里总结规律、提出假设。问题在于，这些自然语言文本本身就漏洞百出。生命科学的论文里，大量结果重复不出来，不少是灌水，还有一部分干脆是造假；而知识库大多又是从这些论文里抽出来的。模型读得再多、总结得再好，学到的也只是“有人这么写过”，而不是“事实就是这样”。可靠的数据在湿实验室里：用同一套方法、同一批试剂测出来，阴性结果留着，每个结果都带着时间戳。这样的数据大多攥在药厂和少数自己做实验的公司手里，后一类公司就是下一节说的投资方向。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>论文到底有多不可靠</summary>

1. **造假已经成了一门产业。** [2023 年被撤回的论文超过 1 万篇，创下纪录，其中 8,000 多篇出自同一家出版商 Hindawi 旗下的期刊](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-03974-8)，Hindawi 这些被撤的论文此前已被引用了 35,000 多次。[2025 年发表在 PNAS 上的一项研究](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2420092122)发现，疑似论文工厂（批量炮制假论文、出售署名的组织）的产出每一年半就翻一番，而整个科学文献每十五年才翻一番。
2. **撤稿只抓到了一小部分。** [一项研究筛查了 20,621 篇生物医学论文，发现其中 3.8% 有不当重复使用的图片，至少一半看起来是故意篡改](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/mbio.00809-16)，而这项研究只查了一种错误。[一项经济学分析估计，超过一半的临床前研究无法重复，仅在美国，每年花在这些研究上的钱就有约 280 亿美元](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.1002165)。前面讲过的安进和拜耳的重复实验也指向同一个结论：大多数已发表的发现，一旦有人想拿它来做项目，就站不住脚。
3. **一篇论文，能把一个领域带偏将近二十年。** [2006 年发表在 Nature 上的一篇论文](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04533)报告说，一种特定的淀粉样蛋白聚合体 Aβ*56 会损害记忆。它成了阿尔茨海默病研究里被引用最多的论文之一，带动了大量后续研究和经费。2024 年 6 月，它因为图片被篡改而撤稿，成了有史以来被引用最多的撤稿论文。任何从这个领域的文献里学习的模型，都把这个结果当成了事实。
4. **模型读文献，会把错误一起学进去。** 文字没法告诉模型，哪些结果是编的，哪些从没被重复过，哪些实验失败了却从没写出来。从论文里搭出来的知识图谱，把一句论断变成一条边；一句话被引用一百次，看起来就像可信了一百倍，哪怕每一次引用都追溯到同一个有问题的实验。读得再多，也只是把同一个错误多抄几遍。
5. **湿实验数据为什么可信。** 湿实验数据也有噪声：前面说过，同一个化合物测两次，结果可能差一倍。区别在于，这种噪声可以测量。方法是固定的，对照组同时在跑，失败会被记下来，任何结果都可以再做一遍。礼来的 TuneLab 和强生的抗体设计挑战赛（下一节会讲）都坚持用自家实验室测的数据，也是这个原因。

</details>

## 什么值得投

药企不愿意把太多内部数据交出去，更希望通过合作，用自己的数据建自己的模型。外人既然拿不到药企的数据，值得投的公司就得满足两条：能快速、低成本地产生自己的金标准数据（gold-standard data，可信度最高、可以拿来当基准的实验数据），而且所在领域确实能做出药。

这就排除了这个领域里的很大一部分。做软件服务式 AI、做通用生物模型的公司日子会很难过，因为 Anthropic 这类大模型公司已经在直接和大药企合作，药企的这类需求，直接找大模型公司就能满足。专有模型不如专有数据，数据能反复迭代，最后做出自己的药。换句话说，值得投的公司，是由自己的湿实验室、最终由自己的临床试验来打分的公司。下面要讲的礼来 TuneLab，就是一家药企自己押的这一注。

<details class="more" markdown="1">
<summary>实践中看到的</summary>

1. **AI 已经能帮着做实验，却还替代不了判断。** 已经有公司用 AI 做实验设计和实验排序，帮了很大的忙；不过在决策和判断上，AI 仍然不行。一家正在做随机对照 II 期试验的公司，在数据库锁定（从这一刻起，试验数据就不能再改）之前的几个月里，和 Anthropic 有很深的合作。
2. **生物技术最难的是学术成果的转化，而转化本质上是可重复性问题。** 大多数学术成果没法直接搬进药物项目，这和前面讲到的那些已发表结果重复不出来，是同一个问题。一个办法是和愿意多走一步的学术创始人深度合作：他们的实验室在论文发表后还会继续打磨，这更像工程，所以交到公司手里的东西，比一般实验室拿出来的成熟得多。
3. **这样成立的公司，起点就高得多。** 实验室的教授本人或他的学生创办公司时，技术虽然还没走到 IND（向 FDA 申请开始人体试验）这一步，但已经做过几个月的研究和几轮先导优化，不再只是论文里的那个版本，公司也就好办得多。
4. **礼来的 TuneLab，是药企亲自下场做的数据生意。** 礼来 [2025 年 9 月推出 TuneLab，模型建在它自己的药物处置（药在体内怎么吸收、分布、代谢和排出）、安全性和临床前数据之上，涉及几十万个分子，背后是超过 10 亿美元的研究投入](https://investor.lilly.com/news-releases/news-release-details/lilly-launches-tunelab-platform-give-biotechnology-companies)。Biotech 可以用这些模型，代价是[贡献自己的数据，通过联邦学习来改进模型](https://www.bio-itworld.com/news/2026/06/30/anthropic-launches-claude-science--illumina's-spatial-insights-tool--wellcome-sanger-institute--google-deepmind-ai-consortium)；联邦学习能用各方的数据训练模型，却不需要把数据挪走或公开。渠道铺得很快：TuneLab 已经接进了 [Benchling，它的客户有 1,300 多家 biotech](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/benchling-and-lilly-tunelab-partner-to-democratize-access-to-ai-models-for-scientists-everywhere-302655983.html)，也接进了 [CDD Vault](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/collaborative-drug-discovery-partners-with-lilly-tunelab-to-make-lilly-aiml-models-available-in-cdd-vault-302777087.html)，Charles River（CRL）还为参与的公司提供检测服务。TuneLab 本身也[挂在礼来的 Catalyze360 项目下面，旁边就是礼来的风险投资部门](https://www.biopharmatrend.com/post/1363-lilly-offers-biotechs-access-to-ai-models-trained-on-1b-in-proprietary-drug-discovery-data)。于是礼来既守住了自己的数据，又能在看不到别人数据的情况下从中受益，还能提前看清哪些 biotech 值得投、值得买。最后这一点，可能比模型本身更值钱。
5. **TuneLab 做的仍然是便宜的那一段。** 按它自己的说法，这些模型帮参与的公司更快地筛掉候选分子。这有用，而且由真实实验来打分，但它落在第一次进入人体之前那百分之九里，碰不到靶点在人身上到底要不要紧。另外还有两个风险。一家 biotech 把数据喂给一个很可能来收购它的公司，迟早会对共享多少数据有所保留。而联邦学习在实验做法各不相同的实验室之间到底能带来多少提升，目前还没有公开的、事先登记的证据。还有一层影响：一家药企用花了十亿美元攒下的数据训练出模型，再开放给 biotech 用，不收钱只收数据，卖性质预测模型的公司就会直接感到价格压力。Anthropic 这类大模型公司和 TuneLab 这类性质预测模型是互补的：大模型管流程，性质预测模型给分子打分，可两者都不做那些最花钱的决策。
6. **强生的做法，就是买家自己出卷子。** 投资人现在想看的，是能自己跑“设计、构建、测试”循环的公司：能把 AI 融进自己的系统，更重要的是能自己产生数据。拿公开数据集做基础模型的公司到处都是，拉不开差距。强生（JNJ）2025 年 10 月发起了一个[计算抗体设计挑战赛](https://cttc.co/node/624)。据业内人士介绍，参赛的十几家抗体设计公司针对三个靶点、每个靶点各设计约 100 条序列，由强生自己的实验室来测，胜出者有机会和强生合作。结果是，强生手里有了全球十几家头部蛋白设计 AI 公司在同一批靶点上的对比数据。参赛公司自己从来没有过这样的基准，都想要这份数据，可数据在强生手里。
7. **数据要独特，不只是要大。** 在最近一场 AI 生物技术公司创始人的讨论里，有几个说法挺实在。第一，模型懂 DNA，不等于懂疾病。从 DNA 到 RNA、蛋白质、细胞，再到患者的结局，链条很长，现有的数据不够直接端到端地预测，分层建模再连起来更务实。同样，理解致病变异也不等于理解疾病。第二，独特的生物学表型，有时比单纯堆样本更有启发。比如有家公司研究冬眠动物的心脏在损伤后怎么修复，从中找心衰治疗的新线索。第三，模型最终要预测的是人体对治疗的反应，把序列预测得更好只是其中一步。第四，药企以后可能更需要药物资产，对纯技术平台的兴趣会变小。AI 也该去探索传统方法够不着、但靶点已经有临床验证的化学空间，别只在已知的化学空间里卷效率。最后，有人认为蛋白质语言模型这一波已经趋于成熟，下一波机会可能在小分子，但模型和数据到底值多少钱，终究要靠商业结果来证明。

</details>

## 谁是真英雄，谁是噱头

判断标准只有一个：谁来给成果打分。真英雄由外部打分：临床试验里的患者、签约就付现金的买家，或者真把分子做出来、测出来的湿实验室。噱头则自己给自己打分：靠公布总额，靠 BP 里的一页管线，靠“AI 发现的药”这块牌子，或者靠一个自己挑的 benchmark。按这个标准，AI 制药里做成了事的，是针对具体问题、在实验室里检验过的物理和化学计算；价值握在出钱做临床、决定什么时候停的那些人和公司手里。以 AI 制药之名卖出去的东西，很多只是挂在这些工作外面的一块牌子。
